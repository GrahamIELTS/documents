Language point: parallel expressions

Why this decides your Reading score

Strong candidates recognise and use parallel expressions: two ways of saying the same thing using completely different words. They include synonyms, idioms and negative structures.

In B2 First, the Reading and Listening papers almost never repeat the words of the text in the question. Part 4 of Use of English (key word transformations) tests the same skill directly.

C1Negative structures and attitude

Negative structures are used to express an attitude as well as to avoid repetition. They let a speaker criticise, hedge or concede without ever saying anything directly.

"I'm not saying I've never read one, but I don't remember the title."
What attitude is this, and how would you say it simply?

Attitude: cautious and self-protective. The speaker does not want to claim they have read a graded reader, and does not want to admit they have not.

Simply: "I may have read one, but I can't remember which."

How the clauses connect: the two halves are joined by contrast linkers — but, however, although, even though. The first clause concedes, the second takes something back.

Now explain the attitude in these two sentences and rewrite the information simply.

  1. "I don't doubt that it's an unsuitable story for kids, even though I enjoyed it when I was younger."
    Model answer

    Attitude: conceding a point they would rather not concede. The speaker accepts the criticism but defends their own experience.

    Simply: "It probably isn't suitable for children, but I liked it when I was young."

  2. "He's not actually refusing to take part in the activity. However, I wouldn't describe him as excited to do the activity."
    Model answer

    Attitude: polite, diplomatic criticism. Understatement is used so that the speaker never says anything openly negative.

    Simply: "He'll take part, but he isn't keen."

Use it yourself

Build the pattern: I'm not saying ... , but ...  |  I wouldn't describe it as ...  |  I don't doubt that ... , even though ...  |  It's not that I ... , it's just that ...

The difference in one line each

glimpse = see for a very short moment. peer = look with difficulty. gaze / stare = look for a long time (stare is ruder). glance = look very quickly. spot = see something you were looking for. witness = see something important happen. observe = watch carefully.

We see without trying, but we look at, watch and read about on purpose. In the same way we hear without trying but we listen to on purpose. Examiners test this pair constantly.

The rule that catches candidates out

Gradable adjectives take very, quite, a bit, rather, mildly. Extreme adjectives take adverbs meaning 100%: absolutely, completely, thoroughly, utterly, totally. So very frightened is fine, but very petrified is not. Really works with both.

Stage five

Exam focus: Reading Part 7

What Part 7 actually asks you to do

Paper 1 contains both Use of English and Reading parts. The final reading task is Part 7: one long text or up to six shorter texts, with ten questions. You match each question with the section of text that contains the answer. Each correct answer is worth 1 point.

The trap: the question never uses the words of the text. "A work that is still fresh" will appear in the text as "just as entertaining now as it was in 1887". You are matching meaning, not words.

Method: read the questions first. Skim all sections. Then, for each question, find the exact phrase in the text that proves it, and underline it. If you cannot underline it, you have not found the answer yet.

Classic Ghost Stories

A bookseller reviews five of the best, and scariest, graded readers for B1/B2 students. For questions 43–52, choose from sections A–E. The sections may be chosen more than once.

A

Set around and after the famous seventeenth-century witch trials in Salem, Massachusetts, this classic nineteenth-century novel is possibly more a tale of horror than a ghost story. After a land dispute with a neighbour, Matthew Maule is sentenced to death for witchcraft, but before he dies he curses the Pyncheon family who accused him. Unexplained deaths and financial losses follow, and several generations of the family suffer. The real star of the story is undoubtedly the house itself. It is still standing in Salem, and tourists and residents alike have reported ghostly visions there. If you are planning a visit to the area, you should definitely arrange to see it. Why not buy the audio version as well, and listen to it while you are travelling?

The House of the Seven Gables by Nathaniel Hawthorne, adapted for intermediate readers.
B

When an American family moves into an English country house, they refuse to be put off by the increasingly terrifying appearances of the resident ghost, Sir Simon. This is much more than a classic ghost story, with themes of romance and love, but most of all it is very funny, especially the descriptions of how the children get the better of Sir Simon through a series of practical jokes. Eventually the teenage daughter of the family befriends the ghost and finds a way to bring him peace. Students of intermediate level and all ages will enjoy this — just as entertaining now as it was in 1887, when it was first published. Buy the book, buy the e-book or listen to the audio version, and look out for the many television, film and theatre adaptations of this wonderful story.

The Canterville Ghost by Oscar Wilde, retold for intermediate readers.
C

This is a dark story which has been re-interpreted in many ways since its publication in 1898. Young Flora takes on a job looking after two orphan children at their uncle's remote country house. Over the summer, Flora begins to see two figures around the house and garden, but she realises that they are not visible to any of the other servants, although the children seem to be aware of them. Her concerns that the man and woman she sees pose a danger to the children prove correct, with tragic results. Intermediate students will find this short work to be a real page-turner, and may find themselves staying up late to finish the story once they have started. It is available as an e-book as well but, be warned, reading the words on a screen is just as spooky as reading them on paper.

The Turn of the Screw by Henry James, retold for intermediate readers.
D

Before it was a massively successful musical, this classic tale of a haunted theatre in Paris was a book published in 1910. At the heart of the story is a mysterious masked phantom, obsessed with the beautiful singer Christine. However, she falls for someone else, who struggles to save her from the phantom when he imprisons her. Most readers will be unaware that this is not a work of pure fiction. The events were inspired by a terrible historical accident, when a fire caused a chandelier to fall from the ceiling, killing one person and injuring several others. What is more, the underground lake that features in the story actually exists beneath the Paris Opera House, although it is really a water storage tank. B2 students will have their pick of options with this title, as it is available as a book, an e-book and in an audio version, not to mention the acclaimed musical, still going strong after nearly forty years.

The Phantom of the Opera by Gaston Leroux, retold for upper-intermediate readers.

Upper-intermediate readers who are looking for something a little different will enjoy this collection of six ghost stories from Japan, available only as a paper copy. Some are traditional folk tales: a musician loses his ears to a ghostly samurai, and a spirit who eats the dead is defeated by a clever monk. Modern stories offer a fascinating counterpoint; in fact, the spirits seem even more terrifying in a familiar setting as they encounter children, priests and even gangsters. The author is both an English language teacher and an acclaimed fiction writer. If this book is your cup of tea, you won't want to miss The Lake Beneath the Station by the same author, a collection of chilling love stories.

Japanese Ghost Stories, retold for upper-intermediate readers.

How to tell a ghost story properly

Storytelling is an ancient art, and every culture has its own version of a ghost story. Before you read: what are five important things to do to make a story really frightening?

Now complete each gap with one word. The first letter is given.

Word bank aircontactdarkfactualhandinterrupted introducelistenersoutphysicalspeedtheretorchvolume

First, consider when and where you tell the story. The best places are, of course, d, quiet and secluded, and it is essential to make sure you won't be i.

Second, stick to this simple structure: i the hero, who will have a problem. While the hero tries to find a solution, they meet someone frightening, the bogeyman, who gets upset … very upset. As the bogeyman takes revenge, leave the end of the story open, so that your audience believes the bogeyman is still o t.

Next, relate the story directly to yourself or to your audience. Say it really happened to your friend or your neighbour, and that it took place in your local area. Make the characters like some of your l. If you include lots of f and believable details, they will imagine that the story is really happening to them.

Another tip is to change the way you speak as you tell the story. Start with a quiet, casual a. As the story continues, change your s and v.

You should also use your voice and body to express individual characters. Plan to bring out some props as well, for example a t or a hook h.

Finally, look for someone in your audience who already seems a little jittery. If they are feeling scared, it will be easy to make them jump, so choose the scariest moment and make p c with them.

Part 1 · Reading Part 7

You are going to read a travel writer's guide to five haunted places in South Africa. For questions 43–52, choose from sections A–E. The sections may be chosen more than once.

AThe road to Uniondale

On the Easter weekend of 1968, a young woman called Maria Charlotte Roux was travelling with her fiancé on the road between Willowmore and Uniondale when their car left the tarmac. She died at the scene; he survived. Ever since, drivers crossing that lonely stretch of the Karoo have reported picking up a silent young hitchhiker in dark clothing who is simply not there when they turn round. Motorcyclists describe a sudden weight behind them, and then nothing at all. What makes the accounts hard to dismiss is that the police station in Uniondale has taken statements from soldiers, farmers and lorry drivers who had never heard the story before it happened to them. Sceptics point out that the road is straight, empty and hypnotic after dark, and that tired eyes invent company. Fill up before you leave. There is nowhere to stop for a hundred kilometres, and nobody to help you if you misjudge it.

BThe ship that never lands

The Flying Dutchman is the best known ghost story on this coast. A captain — Hendrick van der Decken, in the version most often retold — swore that he would round the Cape even if it took him until Judgement Day, and the sea took him at his word. The first printed mention of the legend appears in a traveller's memoir of 1790. What lifts it above harbour gossip is a naval log from 1881: thirteen men aboard a British warship, among them a teenage prince who would later be crowned King George V, recorded a glowing ship crossing their bows before it vanished. Scientists now explain such sightings as a superior mirage, in which layers of cold and warm air bend light and lift a distant vessel into the sky. Stand at Cape Point on a bad afternoon, with the horizon shaking in the heat, and you may decide that the explanation is the less believable of the two.

CThe fortress on the parade

Completed in 1679 after thirteen years of building, the Castle of Good Hope in Cape Town is the oldest colonial building still standing anywhere in the country, and its ghosts are as mixed as its history. Governor Pieter van Noodt condemned seven soldiers to hang; one of them cursed him from the gallows, and the governor was found dead in his chair before the day was out. A tall figure in grey has been reported walking the walls. A black dog approaches visitors on the parade ground and disappears before it reaches them. The bell in the tower is said to ring by itself, which is a curious thing for it to do, since it was walled up long ago. You can walk the dungeons on a guided evening tour, and nobody will charge you a cent extra for the footsteps you hear behind you.

DRoom 10, Nottingham Road

The hotel in the KwaZulu-Natal Midlands opened in 1891 as the Railway Hotel, built to serve a station in a settlement that was then almost nothing at all. Its ghost is called Charlotte, and the accounts of who she was do not agree: a chambermaid in some versions, a rather less respectable woman in others, abandoned by a man either way, and dead after a fall from the balcony outside room 10. Whether she fell or was pushed is the part that nobody in the bar will say out loud. What is unusual is how little anyone fears her. Charlotte tidies. Guests come down to breakfast and report that their clothes have been folded, the flowers moved and the bedding straightened while they slept. Room 10 is booked out months in advance, and not by people hoping to be frightened.

EThe village that stopped

Gold was found in Pilgrim's Creek in Mpumalanga in 1873, and within a year some 1,500 diggers were working four thousand claims in a valley that had held nobody at all. When the gold thinned out, the village did not disappear. It was frozen. The whole of Pilgrim's Rest was declared a national monument in 1986, and its corrugated-iron shopfronts and single dusty street are now a museum you can walk through. Guides run ghost tours after dark, past the cemetery on the hill where one grave lies north to south while every other grave around it lies east to west. It is known as the Robber's Grave, and the man in it is said to have been caught stealing from another digger's tent and hanged for it. The contrast is the point. This is a place where the past has been kept deliberately alive, and where the difference between a museum and a haunting starts to feel very thin indeed.

Part 6 · Writing: a review (140–190 words)

Reviews wanted: places with a story

Is there a place near you that people say is haunted, or that has a strong story attached to it? Write us a review describing the place. Explain what the story is, whether the place is worth visiting, and whether visitors from other countries would find it interesting too.

The best reviews will be published on the website next month.

Checklist before you hand it in

Have you answered all three parts of the task? Examiners mark content first.

Have you used at least three items from this lesson? Try: a page-turner, from cover to cover, not my cup of tea, put off, take on, absolutely petrifying, endlessly fascinating, brings back memories.

Have you used one negative structure to give an opinion politely? "I'm not saying it's frightening, but you won't want to walk back alone."

Have you recommended the place, or advised against it, in your last sentence? A review must end with a verdict.
