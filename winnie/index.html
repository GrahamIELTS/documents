The test that settles it

Can a reasonable person disagree with this sentence? If nobody could possibly argue with it, it is not a claim, and it is not a topic sentence. It is an announcement.

Nobody can disagree with "let's talk about the eyesight." It is an instruction, not a position. But a reader could absolutely disagree with "Early smartphone use is damaging children's eyesight before they reach secondary school." That is a claim, and now the rest of the paragraph has a job: prove it.

This matters more at university than it did at school. A university marker reads your first sentence and decides, in that moment, whether the paragraph is going to argue something. If your opening sentence is a signpost, the marker reads the rest of the paragraph looking for the argument instead of following it.

Three things a topic sentence must carry

It mustWhich meansTest
State a positionNot the subject, your view of the subjectCould someone argue back?
Contain the controlling ideaThe one thing the paragraph will prove, named in the sentenceCan you point at the word?
Be capable of being wrongIf it cannot be false, it carries no informationCould evidence disprove it?

You already wrote a real one

Concentration is a skill that needs to be nurtured during early childhood.Your paragraph 3, sentence 2

That is a proper topic sentence. It takes a position, it names the controlling idea (concentration as a trained skill rather than a fixed trait), and a reader could disagree with it. It is sitting in position two, behind "Finally, as for their ability to focus." Move it to the front and the paragraph starts working immediately.

So this is not a skill you lack. It is a habit of putting the signpost first and the argument second. We are swapping the order.

The five moves

A body paragraph that works is a chain. Each link holds the next one up.

MoveJobSounds like
ClaimWhat you are provingEarly screen access weakens children's judgement.
ReasonWhy it would be trueJudgement develops through slow exposure, and feeds are fast.
EvidenceSomething checkableA 2023 study of 8 to 12 year olds found ...
WarrantWhy that evidence proves that claimBecause the children who scored lowest were also the heaviest users, the link is not incidental.
LinkBack to the argumentJudgement, then, is the second casualty of early access.
The move almost everyone skips is the warrant. Research on university argumentative writing finds that writers at every level produce far more framing material, which sets up the topic and draws causal links, than supporting material, which is the checkable part. It also finds that essay quality depends less on how much evidence appears than on whether the evidence and the claim are doing the same job. Adding a statistic changes nothing if you never say what it proves.

You have exactly one piece of evidence

I used to work part-time in one elementary school and noticed that near one-third of the children there have nearsightedness.Your paragraph 2, sentence 4

This is the best sentence in your essay and you walked straight past it. One third is a number. Numbers are checkable. But you never say whether one third is high, whether it is higher than it used to be, or what it has to do with screens. The evidence is sitting there unconnected to the claim it was meant to prove.

That missing sentence is the warrant. It is one sentence. It would have done more for that paragraph than another hundred words of explanation.

This is not a rule about the word "I"

Some disciplines welcome the first person. What matters is not whether you appear in the sentence, it is what is carrying the weight of the argument.

Here is the problem in one line. If your evidence is your childhood, then a reader with a different childhood has an argument exactly as strong as yours, and there is no way to settle it. Personal experience works as one piece of evidence among several. It fails as the only piece, because it cannot be checked and it cannot be argued with.

Look at your last sentence again. "Due to my experience, I can make an educated guess." You are hedging, and the instinct is completely right. You know a single life is thin evidence, and you are warning the reader. That instinct is the beginning of academic writing. The packaging is what lets it down.

The test for a paragraph break

When the thing you are proving changes, the paragraph ends. Not when it feels long. Not when you have written enough. A paragraph is one claim and everything that proves it. The moment you start proving something else, you are in a new paragraph, even if the old one is only three sentences long.

Why this is hard, and it is not carelessness

Mandarin marks definiteness with word order and context, not with a word in front of the noun. Whether a noun is "the dog" or "a dog" is carried by where it sits in the sentence and what the listener already knows. The information is completely there in your original thought. English just gives it a slot, and your first language does not have that slot to copy from. So there is nothing to transfer, and every article has to be built from scratch.

This also explains one for a. When you wrote "in one elementary school", you were carrying across a measure word structure that counts the thing. English uses "a" for that, and saves "one" for when the number genuinely matters. "I worked at one school" means you worked at exactly one and not two.

Three questions, in order

AskIf yesExample
1. All of them, in general?No article with plurals and uncountablesChildren need sleep.
2. One of many, reader does not know which?a / anShe bought a smartphone.
3. Reader knows exactly which one or ones?theThe children I taught were short sighted.

Run them in that order every time. Question three is the one to be strict about: "the" means the reader can identify it. If they cannot, you have not earned "the" yet.

Register is not vocabulary

The common belief is that academic writing means harder words. It does not. It means a different relationship with your reader: more distance, more precision, fewer moves that assume the reader is in the room with you.

Your essay keeps reaching for the reader as though they are sitting in front of you. That is a warm instinct and it makes your writing readable. At university it costs you.

You made the same error twice

They are exposure to screens too early that have a negative impact on their eyesight, awareness and attention.Your paragraph 1, final sentence
But they are too young that do not have an ability to recognize whether the thing is right or not.Your paragraph 2

This is one pattern, not two mistakes. Mandarin has a single structure for "so much of something that a result follows", built around 太 ... 以至於. It maps beautifully onto a sentence shape English does not allow.

English has no "too ... that". That exact combination is the one English refuses. It splits the job across three separate structures, and which one you need depends on whether you are describing a result or an obstacle.

The three structures

MeaningStructureYour sentence, repaired
Degree causes a resultso + adjective + that + clauseThey are exposed to screens so early that their eyesight suffers.
Degree causes a result, around a nounsuch + a/an + adjective + noun + thatIt is such an early exposure that the damage is difficult to reverse.
Excess prevents somethingtoo + adjective + to + verbThey are too young to judge what they see.

Notice the difference between row one and row three. So ... that reports a result that happened. Too ... to reports something that cannot happen. Your second sentence needed row three: the children are not able to judge. Your first needed row one: the result did happen.

Grammatically, "which" attaches to the nearest noun, and the nearest noun is the advantages. Your sentence currently says the advantages affect their eyesight. That is the opposite of your argument, in the last line of the essay.

Take your first "too ... that" sentence and write it three times: once with so ... that, once with such ... that, once with too ... to. You will find one of the three is a slightly different argument. Say which.

They are exposure to screens too early that have a negative impact on their eyesight, awareness and attention.Your paragraph 1, final sentence
How I would write them

so ... that   Children are exposed to screens so early that their eyesight, judgement and attention are all affected.

such ... that   Children now meet screens at such an early age that the effects reach their eyesight, judgement and attention.

too ... to   Children are exposed to screens too early to develop eyesight, judgement and attention normally.

The third one is the different argument. So and such report damage that has happened. Too ... to reports development that is prevented. Those are not the same claim, and in an essay you would have to decide which one you can actually support.

One last thing

You said your struggle is building longer and more complex sentences. Your essay says otherwise. Your average sentence runs to about seventeen words and your final body paragraph averages nearly twenty two. Length is not your problem.

What the research on academic writing development shows is that maturity is carried less by long sentences with many clauses and more by dense noun phrases: an early exposure, a rate that high, the capacity to concentrate. Those pack meaning into fewer words. Long clause chains are actually a feature of conversation, not of academic prose.

So if anyone tells you to write longer sentences, ignore them. Write tighter ones, and put the complexity into the nouns.
