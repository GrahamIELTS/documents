Start here

On being a quiet speaker

You are not a loud talker, and there is no reason you should become one. The test does not measure volume. It does measure length, and that part is worth separating out.

Being quiet and giving short answers are two different things, and only one of them costs marks. A softly spoken candidate who speaks for forty five seconds scores above a loud one who stops after eight.

It is also worth knowing that this is a described pattern rather than a personal failing. Learners from your background are frequently characterised in the research as reticent in class and afraid of being laughed at, and that reticence is specifically noted as working against them in speaking, not in English generally. You are being asked to override a habit that was reasonable everywhere it was formed.

Speaking is not performing. It is paying attention to the person in front of you, and that framing is useful here, because it removes the requirement to be a bigger version of yourself. You only have to be interested in the question.

A practical point about the room: examiners will not ask you to repeat yourself unless they have to, and a quiet voice with clear consonants is easier to follow than a loud voice without them. Articulation matters more than volume, so the drills below are about clarity rather than about projection.

The one thing I will push on is length. Every answer gets a reason and an example, whether it feels necessary or not.

You have not fixed a test date yet, so this plan assumes you want the level to move rather than the calendar to decide.

What is costing you marks

Every item below has a cause, and in almost every case the cause is that Mandarin does something sensible that English does not allow. That matters, because a random error is a problem and a systematic one is a schedule.

1

Reaching for advanced vocabulary

Lexical resource · Big words slowing everything else down
What I heard

You stop mid sentence to search for an impressive word. The search is visible, the sentence loses its shape, and the word that arrives is often not the one that fits.

Why it happens

This is a taught habit rather than a language one. Study materials and word lists reward rarity, so effort goes there. The cost stays invisible until you are timed.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

It damages three of the four quarters at once. Fluency drops because of the pause, grammar drops because the sentence was abandoned, and vocabulary itself drops, because the marking rewards words used naturally and accurately rather than words that are rare. A rare word used slightly wrongly scores below a common word used exactly.

What to do about it
  1. Answer with the first word that is true. Precision beats rarity in the marking.
  2. Build partners, not words. Learn "make a decision", not "decision".
  3. Reserve ambition for the second sentence of an answer, never the first.
2

Word partners

Lexical resource · Right word, wrong partner
What I heard

The vocabulary is correct and the combination is not. "Do a mistake", "very delicious", "open the light", "learn knowledge".

Why it happens

Words are usually learned one at a time against a translation, and the partner does not travel across languages. In Mandarin the natural partner is a different verb, so the sentence is built correctly by Mandarin logic and lands wrongly in English. Error analysis of learner corpora puts the wrong verb at 42 to 46 percent of all collocation errors: the noun you want is usually right, the verb attached to it is not.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Examiners hear it as limited range, which is unfair given how many words you know, and it is what the descriptors describe.

What to do about it
  1. Record new words with their partner and never alone.
  2. Collect the five verbs that appear most in your own answers and learn what each one takes.
  3. When you read, notice the pair rather than the noun.
3

Concrete detail

Lexical resource · General words where specifics score
What I heard

Answers stay at the level of "it is very convenient" and "the environment is good" without the specific thing that makes it convenient or good.

Why it happens

General adjectives are safe and always available. Specific nouns are riskier and score higher, so the safe choice is the expensive one.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Vocabulary is judged on precision. Three general adjectives score below one exact noun.

What to do about it
  1. Add one number, one name or one place to every answer.
  2. Replace "good" and "convenient" with the reason underneath them.
  3. Practise the follow up sentence: "For example, last week..."
4

Making the connection visible

Fluency and coherence · Good details, with the link left for the listener
What I heard

You give a claim and then real, specific detail, and you stop there. The detail is genuinely good. What is missing is the short sentence that says what it proves.

Why it happens

This is a rhetorical norm, not a thinking gap, and it is one of the best documented differences between how an argument is built in your first language and in English. In that tradition the listener is expected to do part of the work of reaching the meaning, and spelling something out too plainly can read as an insult, because it leaves the other person no room. English runs the opposite contract: the burden of clarity sits with the speaker. Studies of argumentative writing by learners with your background find exactly this shape, evidence stacked in a list without the bridging sentence, and find that adding more evidence does not raise the score. Adding the bridge does.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

It sits in fluency and coherence, which is a full quarter. Coherence is not about connectors like "moreover". It is about whether the listener can see why one sentence follows another. Research on this specific pattern found that once a speaker can supply evidence at all, further marks come from explaining it rather than from adding more, so this is the highest value single change available to you.

What to do about it
  1. One extra sentence per answer, starting with "which means" or "so".
  2. Say the claim, give the example, then say why the example proves the claim. Three steps, every time.
  3. When you finish an answer, ask yourself: did I say the obvious part out loud.
5

Everything stated as absolute

Lexical resource · No room left to move
What I heard

Positions arrive as facts: "People must do this." There is no might, tend to, in most cases, or it depends.

Why it happens

Hedging exists in Mandarin and is carried differently, often by particles and by tone rather than by verbs. Translating directly produces English that sounds far more absolute than you intended.

What it costs

Part 3 rewards nuance. Absolute statements are easy to challenge and leave you defending a position you do not actually hold.

What to do about it
  1. Learn six hedges and use two per Part 3 answer: tend to, in most cases, it depends on, arguably, to some extent, might.
  2. Answer one opinion question a day with a deliberate "it depends".
6

The missing BE verb

Grammatical range and accuracy · Am, is, are dropping out
What I heard

"My hometown very beautiful." "The weather cold today." "I very tired yesterday." The meaning is completely clear, and the verb is not there.

Why it happens

In Mandarin an adjective can be the whole predicate. 很 hen plus an adjective is a complete sentence, and no verb is required, so 我很累 is grammatical and finished. English will not allow this: the adjective needs a verb to attach to. You are not forgetting a word. You are applying a correct rule from another language.

What it costs

It is the single most noticeable error in this group, because it appears in short high frequency sentences where the examiner is listening closely. It reads as a basic accuracy problem even when the rest of the grammar is far above that level.

What to do about it
  1. Before an adjective, ask one question: where is the verb.
  2. Drill the six forms out loud daily until they cost nothing: I am, you are, he is, we are, they are, it is.
  3. Record one minute and count the missing ones. The count falls quickly once you can hear it.
7

Passive structures

Grammatical range and accuracy · Rarely used, though the machinery is there
What I heard

Almost everything is active. Where English would say "the building was completed in 2019" you say "they completed the building in 2019", which is correct and gives away a mark for range.

Why it happens

This one comes with good news attached. Your first language has passives and uses them far less, often reserving them for unpleasant events, so the preference you carry into English is for the active. But research comparing bilinguals like you with people who speak only English found that when a passive is called for, you build it the same way they do, responding to the same underlying cues, and the researchers described that ability as looking built in rather than learned. The structure is not missing. The habit of reaching for it is.

What it costs

Grammatical range is half of a quarter of the marking, and it is counted in the variety of structures you use, not in whether you could use them. A speaker who never produces a passive caps that half.

What to do about it
  1. Learn three fixed frames and use one per answer: it is said that, it was built in, it is often thought that.
  2. Exposure primes structure. Read five passive sentences aloud immediately before you practise, and they will start appearing in your own speech.
  3. Task 1 writing is the natural home for this. Describe one chart a day using passives only.
8

Narrative tenses

Grammatical range and accuracy · Everything told in one tense
What I heard

Past stories are told mostly in the present, or in the simple past only, with no shift between what was happening and what happened.

Why it happens

Mandarin does not mark time on the verb. Time is set once, with a word like yesterday or with a particle such as 了, and the verb never changes afterwards. English asks you to re mark time on every single verb. That is a genuinely heavier load, not a harder idea.

What it costs

Grammatical range is measured on variety, not only on correctness. A whole story in one tense caps that half no matter how accurate it is.

What to do about it
  1. Learn three tenses as one set for storytelling: I was doing, I did, I had done.
  2. Tell one true story a day, out loud, sixty seconds, in the past.
  3. Mark the frame first: "Last year, while I was living in..." then continue.
9

Past endings disappearing

Grammatical range and accuracy · Walked, asked, finished losing their ending
What I heard

The tense is chosen correctly and the ending is not heard. "I walk to school yesterday", where you knew it was past.

Why it happens

This is a sound problem wearing a grammar costume. English past endings land as consonant clusters, and clusters of that shape do not occur at the end of a syllable in Mandarin, so the ending is dropped by the mouth rather than by the grammar.

What it costs

It is marked as a grammar error even though you know the rule, which makes it one of the cheapest things on this list to fix.

What to do about it
  1. Practise the ending as a sound, not a rule: walk t, need id, play d.
  2. Slow motion drill: say the sentence at half speed hitting every ending.
  3. Read aloud two minutes daily and exaggerate every final consonant.
10

A, an and the

Grammatical range and accuracy · Missing, added, or swapped
What I heard

"I went to cinema." "The my friend." Articles appear and disappear without a pattern.

Why it happens

Mandarin has no articles at all. There is no equivalent decision to transfer, so every article is a new judgement made in real time, which is why they fail under pressure rather than on paper. Worth knowing that when I sorted my own teaching records, article errors from students who speak your first language were more often over-specification than omission: reaching for "the" on something the listener has not met yet. Almost all published practice material drills the opposite error, so the exercises you have probably already done were aimed somewhere else.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Frequent, and individually small. Worth fixing after the bigger patterns, not before.

What to do about it
  1. Learn it as one question: does the listener already know which one.
  2. Fix the fifteen fixed phrases first: go to school, at home, in the morning, on the internet.
  3. Do not chase perfection here. Accept a percentage improvement and spend the time elsewhere.
11

Short clauses joined by and

Grammatical range and accuracy · Sentences placed side by side
What I heard

"I go there and it is very beautiful and I take many photos and then I go home." Each piece is correct. Nothing shows how they relate.

Why it happens

Mandarin allows clauses to sit next to each other with the relationship left for the listener to infer, and this is normal, high quality style. English carries the relationship in explicit words: because, although, which, after. The information is not missing from your thinking. It is missing from the sentence.

What it costs

This limits grammatical range more than any single error does. Range is counted in clause types, and a chain of short clauses only ever shows one.

What to do about it
  1. Learn five joiners and use one per answer: although, which, because, after, even though.
  2. Take any two of your own sentences and force them into one.
  3. Aim for one long sentence per answer, not for all of them.
12

Countable and uncountable

Grammatical range and accuracy · Many informations, an advice
What I heard

Nouns English treats as uncountable get a plural or an article: informations, advices, a furniture.

Why it happens

Mandarin counts everything with a measure word, so the noun itself carries no information about whether it can be counted. English hides that property inside the noun and expects you to know it word by word. There is no rule to derive. It is a list.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Larger than it looks. When I sorted my own teaching records across 261 student documents, this was the single most widespread grammar pattern I correct, ahead of subject verb agreement and the missing BE verb. It also rises as topics get more abstract, so it lands hardest in Part 3 and Task 2, which is exactly where you are trying to score.

What to do about it
  1. Learn the twelve that matter: information, advice, news, research, equipment, furniture, luggage, knowledge, progress, traffic, weather, work.
  2. Use a measure phrase: a piece of advice, an item of luggage.
13

Prepositions

Grammatical range and accuracy · In, on, at and the rest
What I heard

Prepositions are chosen by meaning rather than by the fixed pattern English uses.

Why it happens

Mandarin maps space and time onto a different set of words, and no rule transfers. In learner corpora prepositions account for around a tenth of collocation errors, which is real but small next to verb choice.

What it costs

Low individually and noticeable in bulk. Worth doing in the background, never as the main project.

What to do about it
  1. Learn them attached to the noun: on Monday, in July, at six.
  2. Fix the ten that appear most in your own speech and ignore the rest for now.
14

Flat intonation

Pronunciation · A narrow pitch range in English
What I heard

Sounds are clear and the melody is level. Sentences finish on the note they started on, so a listener cannot hear which part matters or when you have finished.

Why it happens

Mandarin uses pitch to distinguish words. Pitch is lexical: change it and you change which word you said, so 買 mǎi and 賣 mài differ by nothing else. English uses pitch across the whole sentence instead, to mark emphasis, attitude and completion. Moving your voice freely feels risky because in Mandarin it would break the word. That caution is correct in one language and expensive in the other.

What it costs

Pronunciation is a full quarter of the score and it is not only about individual sounds. Clear sounds with a flat melody sits in the middle of that quarter. There is a band available here that costs no new vocabulary and no new grammar. Worth knowing too: analyses of first language transfer note that flat or falling English intonation can make a speaker be heard as abrupt or uninterested, which affects how a listener rates their credibility. That has nothing to do with what you meant.

What to do about it
  1. Do the voice set below daily. Five minutes, before anything else.
  2. Listen back for melody only, ignoring the words.
  3. Choose one word per sentence to lift. One is enough to change the whole line.
15

Rhythm

Pronunciation · Every syllable given equal time
What I heard

Speech is even and steady, like a metronome, where English rises and falls with strong and weak beats.

Why it happens

This is a structural difference between the two languages rather than a habit. Mandarin is syllable timed: the length of a sentence depends on how many syllables it has, and each one takes roughly the same time. English is stress timed: the length depends on how many stressed syllables there are, and everything between them gets compressed to fit. Speaking English syllable by syllable is not sloppy. It is the correct rhythm of a different language.

What it costs

It is the main reason clear speech can still be tiring to listen to, and it sits directly in the pronunciation quarter.

What to do about it
  1. Shadow thirty seconds of audio daily, copying the beat rather than the words.
  2. Read a sentence tapping only the strong beats, and let the rest fall where it falls.
  3. Deliberately swallow the small words: the, of, to, and, a.
16

Connected speech

Pronunciation · Clean gaps where English runs words together
What I heard

Every word is produced separately and correctly, with an audible boundary between each one. Nothing is wrong and nothing joins.

Why it happens

The clearest description of this compares the two languages to music: Mandarin is staccato, where the boundary between syllables is meant to be heard, and English is legato, where the flow is continuous and the boundaries are deliberately hard to find. Another version of the same image is that a sentence in your first language is beads strung on a thread and an English sentence is water. You are producing beads, accurately, in a language that wants water.

What it costs

It reads as effortful rather than as incorrect, which caps the pronunciation quarter at the middle. It also hurts your listening, because native speakers are linking and you are listening for separate words.

What to do about it
  1. Learn three linking patterns: consonant into vowel (pick it up), vowel into vowel (go on), and the disappearing t (wan(t) to).
  2. Shadow audio and copy the joins rather than the words.
  3. Read one paragraph aloud daily with no gaps at all, deliberately overdone.
17

Weak forms

Pronunciation · Small words given full value
What I heard

To, of, and, for, can, are all pronounced fully and clearly, every time. English reduces them to almost nothing.

Why it happens

Mandarin has no reduced vowels, so there is no equivalent of the schwa to transfer. Every syllable arrives at full quality. English hides most of its grammar in exactly these hollowed out words, which is also why fast English is hard to hear.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

It is the other half of rhythm, and it works in both directions: producing full forms sounds heavy, and expecting full forms makes native speech harder to follow.

What to do about it
  1. Learn five reduced forms and use them: to becomes ta, of becomes uh, and becomes n, for becomes fer, can becomes kn.
  2. Practise the contrast in one sentence: I can go, where can is almost silent, against I CAN go, where it is not.

Never taught out loud

Pronunciation · English learned as a written subject
What I heard

Your reading and grammar knowledge run well ahead of your speaking. You know rules you cannot yet use at speed, and words you have never once said aloud.

Why it happens

This is a syllabus outcome, not an ability one. Exam focused instruction leaves learners largely confined to English on paper, with little systematic pronunciation teaching, and studies of learners from this background name that directly as the reason speaking lags. If you have never been asked to say a word out loud, the fact that you cannot say it smoothly tells you nothing about your ability. It tells you about the lessons.

What it costs

It makes every other item on this list look worse than it is, because it puts your visible performance below your actual knowledge.

What to do about it
  1. Read aloud daily. Anything. This is the single highest return habit for someone in your position.
  2. Say new words out loud the first time you meet them, before you write them down.
  3. Move material you already know from the page into your mouth. That is a much shorter job than learning it.

What you have that helps

These are not encouragement. They are findings from the same research the rest of this document is built on, and they are the reason the list above is shorter than it looks.

You use more word partnerships than native speakers

A corpus study found learners producing more collocations per thousand words than native speakers did, drawn from a smaller pool. You are not avoiding natural word pairs. You are recycling a safe set of them. Widening a pool is a much smaller job than building one.

You already speak for a listener

The habit of leaving space for the other person to draw their own conclusion is a real skill, and in your first language it is the polite and sophisticated choice. English simply runs on the opposite agreement, where the speaker carries the work of being clear. You are not learning to think better. You are learning which agreement you are working under.

The structures are already built

Research comparing bilinguals like you against people who speak only English found that when it comes to building full passive sentences, you do it the same way they do, responding to the same underlying cues, and the researchers described that ability as looking built in rather than learned. Bilinguals simply use fewer of them, because your other language prefers active sentences. This is a habit to switch on, not a skill to acquire.

The knowledge is already in you

You studied English for years as a written subject, with little systematic speaking or pronunciation teaching. That means what looks like a gap in ability is usually a gap in access: the grammar and vocabulary are already stored, they have just never been asked to come out at speed. Getting at what you already own is a far shorter job than acquiring it.

You are doing all of this in your second language

Whatever band you are at, you are being examined on your ability to argue, give examples and handle abstract topics in a language that is not your first. Most people cannot do that at all. The gap in front of you is between you and a target score, not between you and competence.

Waking up your voice

Five minutes, every morning, before any other English. These are not warm ups for the test. They change the range your voice has available, and a wider range is scored in a quarter of the marking that costs no vocabulary and no grammar.

1The countdown

Answer the same question three times, in ninety seconds, then sixty, then forty five. Same content, less time.

Why it works. Fixing the content forces all the improvement into delivery. Speed rises and pausing falls without you thinking about either.

2The slow motion sentence

Say one sentence at half speed, deliberately hitting every ending sound, then say it at normal speed.

Why it works. Endings are where accuracy is won or lost. Slow motion makes them audible to you, and normal speed keeps some of that clarity.

3The playback

Record sixty seconds of yourself answering any question. Listen once for content, then once for melody only, ignoring the words. Then delete it.

Why it works. The second listen is the one that changes things. You cannot hear your own tune while producing it, and you cannot fix what you have never heard. This is for your ears only.

4The shadow

Play thirty seconds of a native speaker and talk along one beat behind, copying the melody and rhythm, not the meaning.

Why it works. It installs English rhythm as a whole pattern instead of as a list of rules about individual sounds.

There are more of these, and the order they are done in matters. That is a first lesson conversation.

Why this plan looks like this

Not opinion. These are the findings the plan is built on, and they are the reason it asks for twenty minutes daily rather than three hours on Sunday.

Attention is a fixed budget

Speaking research describes a trade off between three things you cannot all maximise at once: how complex your language is, how accurate it is, and how fluent it sounds. Push hard on one and the other two drop. This is not a personal weakness. It is how attention works under time pressure.

Three quarters of what I correct is word choice

I went back through my own teaching records: 261 student documents and 16,488 comments I had written. When I sorted every correction by type, about three quarters were lexical, meaning the wrong word rather than a broken rule. Grammar was the remaining quarter, and inside that quarter prepositions outnumbered articles roughly two to one. This is why your plan leads on word partners rather than on grammar drills. It is where the marks actually are.

What the marking actually rewards

Speaking is scored on four equal parts: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. Each is a quarter. A brilliant word list cannot lift a quarter it does not touch, and a broken quarter drags the whole result down.

You already use more word pairs than native speakers do

A corpus comparison found second language learners producing 31.3 collocations per thousand words against 19.4 for native speakers. The learners used more, drawn from a much smaller set: 639 different types against 1628. So the issue is not that you avoid word partnerships. It is that you rotate a small number of safe ones. That is a range problem, and range builds faster than a habit.

Tsai, reported in Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019

Fluent speakers reuse whole chunks

A large share of natural speech is built from ready made sequences rather than assembled word by word. Learners who store more of these sound faster and pause in more natural places, because they are retrieving instead of constructing.

It is almost always the verb

When learner collocation errors were sorted by type, the wrong choice of verb was the largest single category at 42 to 46 percent, ahead of nouns, prepositions and determiners. The noun you want is usually right. The verb you attach to it is where the mark goes.

Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019

Word partners do not fix themselves as your level rises

In a study drawing on ten thousand learner compositions from students with your language background, middle school writers produced 79 percent correct collocations and university writers 83 percent. The difference was not statistically significant. Accuracy with word partners does not improve just because everything else does, which is why it has to be worked on directly rather than waited out.

Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019, TECCL corpus
