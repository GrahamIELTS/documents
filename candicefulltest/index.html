On being stuck

You have taken the test before and the score has not moved. That is a specific situation and it needs a specific answer, not more of the same practice.

A plateau almost never means you have reached a limit. It means the thing that lifted you to your current band is not the thing that lifts you past it. More practice of the same kind produces more of the same result, which is exactly what you have already observed.

There is a good example of this in the research. A study of learner word partnerships found that accuracy with them did not improve significantly between school level and university level students. Some things simply do not come along for the ride as your general level rises. They have to be worked on directly, and if nobody told you which ones, you would have no way of knowing.

What usually changes a plateau is not volume. It is a change in what is being practised. If the last attempt was built on vocabulary, the next one is usually built on structure and delivery. If it was built on speaking more, the next one is usually built on speaking more precisely.

The findings below are the specific things to change. They are not general advice. They are the reason the score stopped moving.

Your test is on 4 February 2027. From your trial lesson that is 22 weeks, or 156 days.

What is already working

This is not politeness. These are the things I do not have to build, which is what makes the rest of the plan short.

Clear pronunciation

Individual sounds are clear and easy to follow. A listener never has to ask you to repeat a word. That is a real advantage and it protects a quarter of your score.

Strong ideas

You have opinions and you can find something to say on almost any topic. Many candidates lose marks because they have nothing to say. That is not your problem, which means the work is about delivery rather than about thinking.

Willing to keep talking

You keep going rather than stopping when something is difficult. That single habit is worth more than it looks, because the marking rewards sustained speech.

Solid grammar foundation

The basic structures are there and correct. What is missing is variety, not knowledge, and variety is quicker to build than knowledge.

Natural conversational tone

You sound like a person talking rather than a person reciting. That is difficult to teach and you already have it.

Comfortable pace

Your speed is easy to follow, neither rushed nor slow. This is worth protecting when the other work starts.

Personality comes through

You are interesting to listen to. The test is a conversation with a human being, and a human being who is enjoying the conversation is a friendlier marker.

What is costing you marks

Every item below has a cause, and in almost every case the cause is that Mandarin does something sensible that English does not allow. That matters, because a random error is a problem and a systematic one is a schedule.

1

Reaching for advanced vocabulary

Lexical resource · Big words slowing everything else down
What I heard

You stop mid sentence to search for an impressive word. The search is visible, the sentence loses its shape, and the word that arrives is often not the one that fits.

Why it happens

This is a taught habit rather than a language one. Study materials and word lists reward rarity, so effort goes there. The cost stays invisible until you are timed.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

It damages three of the four quarters at once. Fluency drops because of the pause, grammar drops because the sentence was abandoned, and vocabulary itself drops, because the marking rewards words used naturally and accurately rather than words that are rare. A rare word used slightly wrongly scores below a common word used exactly.

What to do about it
  1. Answer with the first word that is true. Precision beats rarity in the marking.
  2. Build partners, not words. Learn "make a decision", not "decision".
  3. Reserve ambition for the second sentence of an answer, never the first.
2

Word partners

Lexical resource · Right word, wrong partner
What I heard

The vocabulary is correct and the combination is not. "Do a mistake", "very delicious", "open the light", "learn knowledge".

Why it happens

Words are usually learned one at a time against a translation, and the partner does not travel across languages. In Mandarin the natural partner is a different verb, so the sentence is built correctly by Mandarin logic and lands wrongly in English. Error analysis of learner corpora puts the wrong verb at 42 to 46 percent of all collocation errors: the noun you want is usually right, the verb attached to it is not.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Examiners hear it as limited range, which is unfair given how many words you know, and it is what the descriptors describe.

What to do about it
  1. Record new words with their partner and never alone.
  2. Collect the five verbs that appear most in your own answers and learn what each one takes.
  3. When you read, notice the pair rather than the noun.
3

The same safe words on rotation

Lexical resource · A small set, used often and correctly
What I heard

Good, important, convenient, a lot of. Nothing is wrong. The same twenty words carry every answer, so the range never shows.

Why it happens

This is a rational strategy under pressure: you use what you are certain about. Corpus work finds learners actually produce more word pairs per thousand words than native speakers do, from roughly a third as many different types. The instinct to bind words together is strong. The pool is narrow.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Lexical resource is a quarter of the marking and it is measured on range and precision together. Accurate and narrow sits in the middle of that quarter.

What to do about it
  1. Ban your three most used adjectives for one week. The replacements arrive quickly.
  2. Keep a list of words you understood while reading but would not have produced. That list is your real range.
  3. Once per answer, replace a general word with the specific noun underneath it.
4

Memorised answers

Lexical resource · Prepared scripts that stop working
What I heard

Some answers arrive complete and polished, then the next question breaks the script and the level drops sharply.

Why it happens

Memorising is a rational response to fear, and to an education that rewarded reproducing correct text. The problem is that it is audible: rhythm, vocabulary and fluency all change at the seam, and the drop afterwards is more visible than the polish before it.

What it costs

Examiners are trained to hear this and the answer is discounted. It also removes the chance to score on the one thing memorising cannot fake, which is responding.

What to do about it
  1. Memorise ideas and examples, never sentences.
  2. Prepare five personal stories that can be bent to fit fifty questions.
  3. Practise answering questions you have not seen, badly, on purpose.
5

The point arrives last

Fluency and coherence · Evidence first, position at the end or never
What I heard

You circle a topic, give background and examples, and the position appears at the very end, or is left for the listener to assemble.

Why it happens

This is a described feature of the rhetorical organisation behind your first language, sometimes called indirection: the discourse turns around the subject and views it from several positions before arriving. It has a long formal history, including five centuries of examination essays built on exactly that shape. It is not disorganisation. It is a different organisation, and English expects the reverse order.

What it costs

An examiner marking coherence is listening for the position early. If it comes at the end, the middle of the answer sounds like it is wandering, even when every sentence is relevant.

What to do about it
  1. Answer the question in the first sentence. Then justify it. This will feel blunt and it is correct here.
  2. If you are undecided, say that first: "It depends, and mainly on..." That is still a position.
  3. Practise the reversal deliberately: take an answer you gave and move the last sentence to the front.
6

Answers that stop too early

Fluency and coherence · Correct, complete, and too short
What I heard

You answer the question, accurately, and then stop, because the question has been answered.

Why it happens

This is a conversational norm, not a language gap. In Mandarin conversation, giving a short answer and leaving space for the other person is polite and appropriate. The test inverts that rule: it wants the long turn, and silence is read as inability rather than as courtesy.

What it costs

Fluency and coherence is a quarter of the score and it is partly measured on sustained speech. A short answer gives the examiner nothing to mark.

What to do about it
  1. Three part shape for every Part 3 answer: position, reason, example.
  2. Add "for example" out loud even when it feels unnecessary.
  3. Practise talking for forty five seconds on a boring topic on purpose.
7

Over correcting

Fluency and coherence · Restarting sentences mid way
What I heard

A sentence begins, an error is noticed, and the sentence restarts. Sometimes twice.

Why it happens

The awareness is good and the timing is expensive. Every restart is scored as a break in fluency, and the original error was usually smaller than the repair. Learners who were taught in a system where a written mistake was the thing that cost marks tend to carry that reflex into speech, where the economics are reversed.

What it costs

A repaired sentence costs more than an uncorrected small error. Two repairs cost more than the error was ever worth.

What to do about it
  1. One repair rule: fix it once, then continue whatever happens.
  2. Finish the sentence you started, even badly.
  3. Save the correction for the recording, afterwards.
8

The missing BE verb

Grammatical range and accuracy · Am, is, are dropping out
What I heard

"My hometown very beautiful." "The weather cold today." "I very tired yesterday." The meaning is completely clear, and the verb is not there.

Why it happens

In Mandarin an adjective can be the whole predicate. 很 hen plus an adjective is a complete sentence, and no verb is required, so 我很累 is grammatical and finished. English will not allow this: the adjective needs a verb to attach to. You are not forgetting a word. You are applying a correct rule from another language.

What it costs

It is the single most noticeable error in this group, because it appears in short high frequency sentences where the examiner is listening closely. It reads as a basic accuracy problem even when the rest of the grammar is far above that level.

What to do about it
  1. Before an adjective, ask one question: where is the verb.
  2. Drill the six forms out loud daily until they cost nothing: I am, you are, he is, we are, they are, it is.
  3. Record one minute and count the missing ones. The count falls quickly once you can hear it.
9

Passive structures

Grammatical range and accuracy · Rarely used, though the machinery is there
What I heard

Almost everything is active. Where English would say "the building was completed in 2019" you say "they completed the building in 2019", which is correct and gives away a mark for range.

Why it happens

This one comes with good news attached. Your first language has passives and uses them far less, often reserving them for unpleasant events, so the preference you carry into English is for the active. But research comparing bilinguals like you with people who speak only English found that when a passive is called for, you build it the same way they do, responding to the same underlying cues, and the researchers described that ability as looking built in rather than learned. The structure is not missing. The habit of reaching for it is.

What it costs

Grammatical range is half of a quarter of the marking, and it is counted in the variety of structures you use, not in whether you could use them. A speaker who never produces a passive caps that half.

What to do about it
  1. Learn three fixed frames and use one per answer: it is said that, it was built in, it is often thought that.
  2. Exposure primes structure. Read five passive sentences aloud immediately before you practise, and they will start appearing in your own speech.
  3. Task 1 writing is the natural home for this. Describe one chart a day using passives only.
10

Narrative tenses

Grammatical range and accuracy · Everything told in one tense
What I heard

The simple past is solid. What is missing is the second layer: the past continuous for the background and the past perfect for what came before, which is what turns a list of events into a story.

Why it happens

Mandarin does not mark time on the verb. Time is set once, with a word like yesterday or with a particle such as 了, and the verb never changes afterwards. English asks you to re mark time on every single verb. That is a genuinely heavier load, not a harder idea.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Grammatical range is measured on variety, not only on correctness. A whole story in one tense caps that half no matter how accurate it is.

What to do about it
  1. Learn three tenses as one set for storytelling: I was doing, I did, I had done.
  2. Tell one true story a day, out loud, sixty seconds, in the past.
  3. Mark the frame first: "Last year, while I was living in..." then continue.
11

Conditionals

Grammatical range and accuracy · If I have money, I will buy
What I heard

The real conditional works. The unreal ones flatten into it, so hypothetical Part 3 questions get answered as though they were about the actual future.

Why it happens

Mandarin marks hypothetical meaning with words like 如果 and with context, not by shifting the tense backwards. English signals unreality by moving the verb into the past, which is counter intuitive if your first language never does it.

What it costs

Part 3 is full of hypothetical questions. Answering them in the wrong conditional both loses a range mark and slightly changes what you appear to be claiming.

What to do about it
  1. Drill the second conditional as a fixed frame: if I had, I would.
  2. Answer one "what would happen if" question a day out loud.
  3. Notice the trigger words: would, imagine, suppose. They all take the shifted tense.
12

Question formation

Grammatical range and accuracy · You know where is the station
What I heard

Direct and indirect questions use the same word order.

Why it happens

Mandarin forms questions by adding a particle and leaving the word order alone. English inverts the order for a direct question and then un inverts it inside a longer sentence, which is two opposite rules for one idea.

What it costs

Small in Speaking, where you answer more than you ask. It appears in Part 3 hypotheticals and in writing.

What to do about it
  1. Drill the pair: "Where is it" against "I do not know where it is".
  2. Say five embedded questions a day out loud.
13

Diphthongs

Pronunciation · House and horse, bide and bed
What I heard

Two part vowels flatten into one. House moves towards horse, and bide towards bad or bed.

Why it happens

A diphthong is built from two vowels sliding together, so if either piece is already off target the whole glide lands somewhere else. Because the single vowels in Mandarin sit in different places from their English near equivalents, the errors compound rather than adding up.

What it costs

Moderate, and worth fixing after the single vowels rather than before, since the single vowels are the cause.

What to do about it
  1. Fix the two single vowels first. The diphthong usually corrects itself.
  2. Slow the glide right down, then speed it up: ah ee, ah ee, aye.
14

NG and N

Pronunciation · Thing and thin
What I heard

Word final ng and n collapse together, so thing and thin, or sing and sin, are hard to tell apart.

Why it happens

Both sounds can end a syllable in Mandarin, which is why this one is less common than the other consonant items, but several regional varieties merge them and the merge transfers.

What it costs

Small, and it shows up in the present continuous, which you use constantly, so it accumulates.

What to do about it
  1. The back of the tongue lifts for ng and the tip lifts for n. Say both with the mouth held open to feel which part moves.
  2. Drill the ing ending across ten verbs a day.

Never taught out loud

Pronunciation · English learned as a written subject
What I heard

Your reading and grammar knowledge run well ahead of your speaking. You know rules you cannot yet use at speed, and words you have never once said aloud.

Why it happens

This is a syllabus outcome, not an ability one. Exam focused instruction leaves learners largely confined to English on paper, with little systematic pronunciation teaching, and studies of learners from this background name that directly as the reason speaking lags. If you have never been asked to say a word out loud, the fact that you cannot say it smoothly tells you nothing about your ability. It tells you about the lessons.

What it costs

It makes every other item on this list look worse than it is, because it puts your visible performance below your actual knowledge.

What to do about it
  1. Read aloud daily. Anything. This is the single highest return habit for someone in your position.
  2. Say new words out loud the first time you meet them, before you write them down.
  3. Move material you already know from the page into your mouth. That is a much shorter job than learning it.

What you have that helps

These are not encouragement. They are findings from the same research the rest of this document is built on, and they are the reason the list above is shorter than it looks.

You use more word partnerships than native speakers

A corpus study found learners producing more collocations per thousand words than native speakers did, drawn from a smaller pool. You are not avoiding natural word pairs. You are recycling a safe set of them. Widening a pool is a much smaller job than building one.

The knowledge is already in you

You studied English for years as a written subject, with little systematic speaking or pronunciation teaching. That means what looks like a gap in ability is usually a gap in access: the grammar and vocabulary are already stored, they have just never been asked to come out at speed. Getting at what you already own is a far shorter job than acquiring it.

You already speak for a listener

The habit of leaving space for the other person to draw their own conclusion is a real skill, and in your first language it is the polite and sophisticated choice. English simply runs on the opposite agreement, where the speaker carries the work of being clear. You are not learning to think better. You are learning which agreement you are working under.

The structures are already built

Research comparing bilinguals like you against people who speak only English found that when it comes to building full passive sentences, you do it the same way they do, responding to the same underlying cues, and the researchers described that ability as looking built in rather than learned. Bilinguals simply use fewer of them, because your other language prefers active sentences. This is a habit to switch on, not a skill to acquire.

You are doing all of this in your second language

Whatever band you are at, you are being examined on your ability to argue, give examples and handle abstract topics in a language that is not your first. Most people cannot do that at all. The gap in front of you is between you and a target score, not between you and competence.

Waking up your voice

Five minutes, every morning, before any other English. These are not warm ups for the test. They change the range your voice has available, and a wider range is scored in a quarter of the marking that costs no vocabulary and no grammar.

1The countdown

Answer the same question three times, in ninety seconds, then sixty, then forty five. Same content, less time.

Why it works. Fixing the content forces all the improvement into delivery. Speed rises and pausing falls without you thinking about either.

2The one breath rule

Answer a question in one sentence per breath. When the breath ends, the sentence ends. Take a new breath and start a new one.

Why it works. It stops sentences running on until they collapse, which is the most common cause of grammar breaking down mid answer.

3The slow motion sentence

Say one sentence at half speed, deliberately hitting every ending sound, then say it at normal speed.

Why it works. Endings are where accuracy is won or lost. Slow motion makes them audible to you, and normal speed keeps some of that clarity.

4The playback

Record sixty seconds of yourself answering any question. Listen once for content, then once for melody only, ignoring the words. Then delete it.

Why it works. The second listen is the one that changes things. You cannot hear your own tune while producing it, and you cannot fix what you have never heard. This is for your ears only.

5The shadow

Play thirty seconds of a native speaker and talk along one beat behind, copying the melody and rhythm, not the meaning.

Why it works. It installs English rhythm as a whole pattern instead of as a list of rules about individual sounds.

6The story voice

Read one paragraph of a children’s book out loud, to an imaginary six year old who is losing interest. Exaggerate everything.

Why it works. It removes the fear of sounding excessive. Played back, the exaggerated version almost always sounds normal, which tells you how much room you actually have.

Why this plan looks like this

Not opinion. These are the findings the plan is built on, and they are the reason it asks for twenty minutes daily rather than three hours on Sunday.

Attention is a fixed budget

Speaking research describes a trade off between three things you cannot all maximise at once: how complex your language is, how accurate it is, and how fluent it sounds. Push hard on one and the other two drop. This is not a personal weakness. It is how attention works under time pressure.

Three quarters of what I correct is word choice

I went back through my own teaching records: 261 student documents and 16,488 comments I had written. When I sorted every correction by type, about three quarters were lexical, meaning the wrong word rather than a broken rule. Grammar was the remaining quarter, and inside that quarter prepositions outnumbered articles roughly two to one. This is why your plan leads on word partners rather than on grammar drills. It is where the marks actually are.

What the marking actually rewards

Speaking is scored on four equal parts: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. Each is a quarter. A brilliant word list cannot lift a quarter it does not touch, and a broken quarter drags the whole result down.

You already use more word pairs than native speakers do

A corpus comparison found second language learners producing 31.3 collocations per thousand words against 19.4 for native speakers. The learners used more, drawn from a much smaller set: 639 different types against 1628. So the issue is not that you avoid word partnerships. It is that you rotate a small number of safe ones. That is a range problem, and range builds faster than a habit.

Tsai, reported in Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019

Fluent speakers reuse whole chunks

A large share of natural speech is built from ready made sequences rather than assembled word by word. Learners who store more of these sound faster and pause in more natural places, because they are retrieving instead of constructing.

It is almost always the verb

When learner collocation errors were sorted by type, the wrong choice of verb was the largest single category at 42 to 46 percent, ahead of nouns, prepositions and determiners. The noun you want is usually right. The verb you attach to it is where the mark goes.

Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019

Word partners do not fix themselves as your level rises

In a study drawing on ten thousand learner compositions from students with your language background, middle school writers produced 79 percent correct collocations and university writers 83 percent. The difference was not statistically significant. Accuracy with word partners does not improve just because everything else does, which is why it has to be worked on directly rather than waited out.

Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019, TECCL corpus

Anxiety costs a real band, and it is trainable

Reviews across many studies find a consistent negative relationship between speaking anxiety and speaking performance. Anxiety occupies the working memory that grammar needs. Rehearsal under realistic conditions lowers it, because most of the fear is fear of the unfamiliar.

You were taught English as a written subject

Exam focused instruction leaves learners confined largely to English on paper, with little systematic pronunciation teaching. If your reading and grammar knowledge run well ahead of your speaking, that is the predictable outcome of the syllabus you sat, not a measure of your ability.

Wu, 2022

The shape of a good argument is not universal

The traditional pattern behind your first language builds from evidence towards a point, circling the topic and arriving at the thesis late, or leaving it to be inferred. That is a rhetorical tradition with five centuries of examination history behind it, not a mistake. English expects the point first and the evidence after. Learning the English shape does not mean the other one was wrong.

Chien, on Kaplan 1966 and Connor 1996

Who carries the work of being clear

Research on the rhetorical tradition behind your first language describes a norm where the reader is expected to work to reach the meaning, and where stating something too explicitly can read as an insult, because it leaves the reader no room to think. English academic and test discourse runs on the opposite contract: the burden of clarity sits with the speaker. Neither is better. They are different agreements about who does the work, and the test is marked under the English one.

Chien, on Kaplan and Connor

The gap between having evidence and using it

A study of 542 timed argumentative essays by undergraduates with your language background found the pattern changes with level. Weaker texts are assertion heavy and example light. Middle texts have plenty of evidence but stack it in a list without the bridging sentence that says why it counts. Only the strongest texts make that link explicit. Once you can supply evidence, further marks come from explaining it, not from adding more.

Yang, 2025

Part 3 is where corrections thin out

In my own records, Part 3 draws about half the corrections per comment that Parts 1 and 2 do, while the amount of teaching I do holds steady. Two readings are possible: either abstract answers genuinely produce fewer isolable errors, or the errors get absorbed into the argument and stop being separable. Either way it means Part 3 needs a different kind of preparation, built around shape rather than around accuracy.

GrahamIELTS teaching corpus, 261 documents

Quiet is not the same as unable

A long standing observation in this field is that learners from this background are often described as quiet and reluctant in class, afraid of mistakes, of laughter, of being wrong in front of others, and that this works against them in speaking assessments specifically. The reticence is a response to how speaking has been taught and marked. It is not evidence about your English.

Xu, cited in Zhang and Yin, 2009

You cannot fix what you cannot hear

Change begins with noticing the gap between what you said and what a proficient speaker would say. Recording yourself is not vanity. It is the only way to hear your own gap, because in the moment of speaking your attention is elsewhere.

Your first language is not the enemy

Every predictable error in this report has a cause in the structure of your first language. That is what makes it predictable, and predictable is exactly what can be trained. A random error is a problem. A systematic one is a schedule.

Practice moves work off the front desk

A structure you have to think about consumes working memory. The same structure, practised until it is automatic, consumes almost none. The aim of drilling is not to learn the rule. It is to stop the rule costing you anything.

Your English machinery is not second hand

A study comparing English and Mandarin bilinguals against people who speak only English found the bilinguals build full passive sentences in the same way, responding to the same underlying cues, and described that ability as looking built in rather than merely learned from exposure. Bilinguals used fewer passives overall, because Mandarin prefers active sentences, but the machinery was the same. What is missing is a habit, not an ability.

Luo, 2023

Structures you have just heard become available

Speakers reuse a structure they have recently encountered, with different words. Hear a passive and you are more likely to produce one, even on an unrelated topic. This is why the fix for a structure you rarely use is not a grammar rule. It is exposure immediately before you speak.

Bock, 1986; Luo, 2023

Range is measured in clauses, not in long words

Studies tracking learner development find growth shows up as more subordination and more varied clause types, not as longer vocabulary. Two ideas joined properly score above two ideas placed side by side.

Saying it out loud marks it

Material spoken aloud is remembered better than material read silently. Producing it makes it distinctive. Silent reading feels efficient and is not.

You cannot say a sound you cannot hear

Where a sound distinction does not change meaning in your first language, you may not register it at all. As one analysis puts it, if you cannot hear it you cannot even attempt it, so perception has to be fixed before production. This is why the work starts with listening in pairs rather than with your own mouth.

Zhang and Yin, 2009

Spread it out, do not stack it

Reviews of hundreds of studies find the same thing: practice spread across days beats the same total time in one block, often by a wide margin. Twenty minutes on six days does more than two hours on Sunday.
