The because engine, real reasons only

Adapted from Tim David·Magic Words·former professional magician

Exercise 14, page 16Tool 22, The because engineMoves: Fluency and coherence, and grammar range and accuracy
Why this one, for you

Candicefulltest, I took this off making the connection visible, because you are already doing the hard half. The claim is there and the evidence is real, and the only thing missing is the sentence that says what the evidence proves. This exercise is that sentence, drilled on its own, four times.

What the play is

Ellen Langer, the first woman ever tenured in psychology at Harvard, had someone ask to jump a photocopier queue. With no reason, some people agreed. With a real reason, most did. With an empty reason, because I need to make copies, almost as many agreed as with the real one. The word alone satisfied a listener who was not really listening.

Your listener is the exact opposite. Grading the reason is their job. So the trick that works in a queue fails on a mark sheet, and an empty because reads as precisely what it is: filler wearing a logic costume. This is the inversion worth carrying: the thing that helps everywhere else hurts here, and it hurts at the moment it feels most helpful.

A loaded because pays twice. It is a subordinate clause, which is grammar range, and it carries development, which is fluency and coherence. Two quarters of the mark from one sentence, on the single condition that the clause contains something new. Give every because cargo: a mechanism, how the thing works; a consequence, what follows from it; or a condition, when it holds and when it does not.

What to do
  1. Each claim below is followed by a hollow because. Rewrite each one with cargo.
  2. Use a mechanism (because it changes how...), a consequence (because without it...), or a condition (because whenever...).
  3. Rotate the three types. Do not use mechanism four times.
  4. Test each rewrite by covering the claim: does the because clause still tell me something on its own?
One claim, worked through

"Public parks matter because they are important to cities." That because carries nothing, it restates the claim in different words. With a mechanism: "public parks matter because they are the only free room a city has, so they are the one place where people who would never meet end up in the same space." The claim did not change. The clause now has something in it, and it is a subordinate clause the examiner can mark.

The exercise, Exercise 14
Public parks matter because they are important to cities.
Students should learn history because it is useful knowledge.

Then speak it, on your own

The surroundings because drill. On your commute, make five claims about what you can see, each with a loaded because. "This bus stop is badly placed, because the shelter faces the afternoon sun, so nobody uses the bench after two." Mechanism, consequence, condition, rotating. The street is an endless supply of Part 3 questions in disguise.

How much of this there is. The playbook and the workbook run to just over a hundred pages between them: fifty seven tools and twenty eight exercises. You are not going to be handed all of it at once, and you should not want to be.

The rest arrive in the same way this one did, chosen off what I hear rather than off the contents page, and aimed more narrowly each time as I learn where you slip.

None of this is exam material, either. Naming a thing exactly rather than generally, giving a reason that carries real weight, holding two sides of an argument at once, landing the end of a sentence so it sounds decided: those are worth as much in a meeting, an interview, or a first conversation with someone you have just met, as they are in the test. The test is simply the reason we are finally training them.
