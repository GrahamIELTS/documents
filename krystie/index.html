On feeling nervous about this

You told me you feel nervous about the test. That is worth answering properly rather than brushing past, because nervousness is not a personality problem here. It is a measurable cost, and it is trainable.

Research across a large number of studies finds a consistent negative relationship between speaking anxiety and speaking performance. The mechanism is simple: anxiety occupies working memory, and grammar needs the same working memory. You are not less capable when you are nervous. You have less capacity free.

Four principles guide how I will approach speaking with you.

The first: speaking is not performing. It is paying attention to the person in front of you. Nervousness comes almost entirely from watching yourself. Move the attention onto the examiner, onto the actual question, onto what you are describing, and there is less of you left over to be nervous with.

The second: you do not need to win the conversation. You need to keep it alive. Every answer is a pass, not a shot. This removes the idea that any single sentence can ruin the test, which is the belief doing most of the damage.

The third: say the small true thing rather than the big impressive thing. The small true thing is always available, always accurate, and scores higher than the impressive sentence that collapses halfway.

The fourth: do not apologise before you begin. No "my English is not good", no nervous laugh before the answer. It costs you nothing to remove and it changes how the whole conversation is heard.

Practically, most of the fear is fear of the unfamiliar, and the unfamiliar is the only part I can remove directly. By the time you sit the test you will have done the format enough times that it is boring. Boring is the goal.

What is costing you marks

Every item below has a cause, and in almost every case the cause is that Mandarin does something sensible that English does not allow. That matters, because a random error is a problem and a systematic one is a schedule.

1

Word partners

Vocabulary · Right word, wrong partner
What I heard

The vocabulary is correct but the combination is not. "Do a mistake", "very delicious", "open the light", "learn knowledge".

Why it happens

Words are usually learned one at a time with a translation, and the partner does not travel across languages. In Mandarin the natural partner is a different verb, so the sentence is built correctly by Mandarin logic and lands wrongly in English.

What it costs

Examiners hear it as limited range, which is unfair given how many words you know, but it is what the descriptors describe.

What to do about it
  1. Record new words with their partner and never alone.
  2. Collect the four or five verbs that appear most in your own answers and learn what each one takes.
  3. When you read, notice the pair rather than the noun.
2

Concrete detail

Vocabulary · General words where specifics score
What I heard

Answers stay at the level of "it is very convenient" and "the environment is good" without the specific thing that makes it convenient or good.

Why it happens

General adjectives are safe and always available. Specific nouns are riskier and score higher, so the safe choice is the expensive one.

What it costs

Vocabulary is judged on precision. Three general adjectives score below one exact noun.

What to do about it
  1. Add one number, one name or one place to every answer.
  2. Replace "good" and "convenient" with the reason underneath them.
  3. Practise the follow up sentence: "For example, last week..."
3

Narrative tenses

Grammar · Everything told in one tense
What I heard

Past stories are told mostly in the present, or in the simple past only, with no shift between what was happening and what happened.

Why it happens

Mandarin does not mark time on the verb. Time is set once, with a word like yesterday or with a particle, and then the verb never changes. English asks you to re mark time on every single verb, which is a genuinely heavier load, not a harder idea.

What it costs

Grammatical range is a quarter of the score and range means variety, not just correctness. A whole story in one tense caps that quarter no matter how accurate it is.

What to do about it
  1. Learn three tenses as one set for storytelling: I was doing, I did, I had done.
  2. Tell one true story a day, out loud, sixty seconds, in the past.
  3. Mark the frame first: "Last year, while I was living in..." then continue.
4

Third person s

Grammar · He go, she like
What I heard

The verb stays in its base form when the subject is he, she or it.

Why it happens

Mandarin verbs do not change for the subject at all. One form serves everybody, which is a simpler and arguably better system. English asks for an ending that carries no meaning, so it is the first thing dropped under load.

What it costs

Very visible to examiners because it is taught early. It reads as a lower level than you are.

What to do about it
  1. Drill he, she and it separately from I, you and they.
  2. Say five sentences a day about one other person out loud.
  3. When you self correct, correct the whole sentence, not the word.
5

Short clauses joined by and

Grammar · Sentences placed side by side
What I heard

"I go there and it is very beautiful and I take many photos and then I go home." Each piece is correct. Nothing shows how they relate.

Why it happens

Mandarin allows clauses to sit next to each other with the relationship left for the listener to infer, and this is normal, high quality style. English carries the relationship in explicit words: because, although, which, after. The information is not missing from your thinking. It is missing from the sentence.

What it costs

This is what limits grammatical range more than any single error does. Range is measured in clause types, and a chain of short clauses only ever shows one.

What to do about it
  1. Learn five joiners and use one per answer: although, which, because, after, even though.
  2. Take any two of your own sentences and force them into one.
  3. Aim for one long sentence per answer, not for all of them.
6

Hedging and modality

Grammar · Everything stated as absolute
What I heard

Positions arrive as facts: "People must do this." There is no might, tend to, in most cases, or it depends.

Why it happens

Hedging exists in Mandarin but is carried differently, often by particles and tone rather than by verbs. Translating directly produces English that sounds more absolute than you intended.

What it costs

Part 3 rewards nuance. Absolute statements are easy to challenge and leave you defending a position you do not hold.

What to do about it
  1. Learn six hedges and use two per Part 3 answer: tend to, in most cases, it depends on, arguably, to some extent, might.
  2. Answer one opinion question a day with a deliberate "it depends".
7

Flat intonation

Delivery · A narrow pitch range in English
What I heard

Sounds are clear and the melody is level. Sentences finish on the same note they started, so a listener cannot hear which part matters or when you have finished.

Why it happens

Mandarin uses pitch to distinguish words. Pitch is lexical: change the pitch and you change the meaning of the word. English uses pitch at the level of the sentence instead, to mark emphasis, attitude and completion. Moving your voice freely feels risky because in Mandarin it would break the word, so the safest strategy is to keep it level. That strategy is correct in one language and costly in the other.

What it costs

Pronunciation is a full quarter of the score, and it is not only about individual sounds. Clear sounds with a flat melody sits in the middle of that quarter. There is a band available here that costs no new vocabulary and no new grammar.

What to do about it
  1. Do the voice set below daily. Five minutes, before anything else.
  2. Record and listen for melody only, ignoring the words.
  3. Choose one word per sentence to lift. One is enough to change the whole line.
8

Answers that stop too early

Fluency · Correct, complete, and too short
What I heard

You answer the question, accurately, and then stop, because the question has been answered.

Why it happens

This is a conversational norm, not a language gap. In Mandarin conversation, giving a short answer and letting the other person continue is polite and appropriate. The test inverts that rule: it wants the long turn, and silence is read as inability rather than as courtesy.

What it costs

Fluency and coherence is a quarter of the score and it is partly measured by sustained speech. A short answer gives the examiner nothing to score.

What to do about it
  1. Three part shape for every Part 3 answer: position, reason, example.
  2. Add "for example" out loud even when it feels unnecessary.
  3. Practise talking for forty five seconds on a boring topic on purpose.

Waking up your voice

Five minutes, every morning, before any other English. These are not warm ups for the test. They change the range your voice has available, and a wider range is scored in a quarter of the marking that costs no vocabulary and no grammar.

1The one breath rule

Answer a question in one sentence per breath. When the breath ends, the sentence ends. Take a new breath and start a new one.

Why it works. It stops sentences running on until they collapse, which is the most common cause of grammar breaking down mid answer.

2The countdown

Answer the same question three times, in ninety seconds, then sixty, then forty five. Same content, less time.

Why it works. Fixing the content forces all the improvement into delivery. Speed rises and pausing falls without you thinking about either.

3The slow motion sentence

Say one sentence at half speed, deliberately hitting every ending sound, then say it at normal speed.

Why it works. Endings are where accuracy is won or lost. Slow motion makes them audible to you, and normal speed keeps some of that clarity.

4The siren

Slide your voice from the lowest comfortable note to the highest and back down on one breath, on a hum, then on the word "no". Thirty seconds.

Why it works. Most speakers use a fraction of the range they own. This finds the edges, so normal speech sits in the middle of a wide range instead of at the top of a narrow one.

There are more of these, and the order they are done in matters. That is a first lesson conversation.

Why this plan looks like this

Not opinion. These are the findings the plan is built on, and they are the reason it asks for twenty minutes daily rather than three hours on Sunday.

Word partners, not single words

Corpus studies of learners with a Mandarin or Cantonese first language find that errors cluster in which words go together rather than in which words are known. The word is right and the partner is wrong. Examiners hear this as limited range even when the vocabulary is large.

Fluent speakers reuse whole chunks

Studies of natural speech show a large share of it is built from ready made sequences rather than assembled word by word. Learners who store more of these chunks sound faster and pause in more natural places, because they are retrieving instead of constructing.

Answers need a shape

Research on learners from a Mandarin or Cantonese background finds a pattern of stating a position and then moving on, where higher scoring answers state, support, and illustrate. The knowledge is there. The habit of unpacking it is what is missing.

What the marking actually rewards

Speaking is scored on four equal parts: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. Each is a quarter of the score. A brilliant word list cannot lift a quarter it does not touch, and a broken quarter drags the whole result down.

Your first language is not the enemy

Every predictable error in this report has a cause in the structure of your first language. That is what makes it predictable, and predictable is exactly what can be trained. A random error is a problem. A systematic one is a schedule.

Range is measured in clauses, not in long words

Studies tracking learner development find that growth shows up as more subordination and more varied clause types, not as longer vocabulary. Two sentences joined properly score above two sentences placed side by side.
