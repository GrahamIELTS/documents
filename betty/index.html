From your trial

What I heard

Three things. Each one has a cause, and the cause is not you.

1

You are spending your whole mind on finding words

This was the clearest thing in the recording. You have a large vocabulary and you want to use it, so while you are speaking you are also searching: for the better word, the more advanced word, the one that will impress. Then you bend the sentence to fit the word you found.

It costs you twice. You hesitate in the middle of sentences, which is the kind of pause an examiner actually notices. And the advanced word often arrives slightly wrong, sitting in a sentence that was rebuilt around it, so it sounds less natural than the simple word you passed over.

Why this happens, and it is not a discipline problem: speaking uses a limited pool of attention. Research that measures this directly has found that learners who reach for elaborate vocabulary produce measurably more filled pauses, and that learners using more complex sentence structures make significantly more errors. You cannot push one dial up without the others moving down. That is not a personal failing. It is how attention works, and it has a name and a body of evidence behind it.
The important part: in the same research, the trade-off disappeared completely by the third attempt at the same task. Once the material was familiar, learners could hold complexity, accuracy and fluency at the same time. Familiarity is what buys you back your attention. That is the entire logic of how I am going to train you.
2

Your voice stays on one note

Your pronunciation is clear. Individual sounds are not your problem, and I want to be direct about that so you do not go and work on the wrong thing. What is missing is movement. Your pitch sits in a narrow band and stays there, so a sentence you find genuinely interesting sounds the same as one you do not care about.

This costs you directly on the scoresheet. The pronunciation criterion is built on the word range. Band 6 is described as using a range of pronunciation features with mixed control. Band 8 is a wide range. Intonation is one of those features, so a flat voice narrows your range and caps that score no matter how clean your consonants are.

Why this happens, and it is genuinely not your fault: in Mandarin, pitch is doing a different job. It distinguishes words. In English, pitch works across the whole sentence to show what matters and what you think about it. Research comparing the two found that native English speakers use a significantly wider pitch range to mark the important word in a sentence, while Mandarin-speaking learners of English did not do this at all, even when the important word was printed in bold in front of them. They used loudness instead, which English speakers do not use for that job.
Here is the encouraging half of that finding: the same speakers do use pitch this way in Mandarin. The ability is already in you and it is already working. It simply has not transferred into English yet, because nobody has ever asked it to. That is a much smaller problem than learning something new, and it is why the drills below work quickly.
3

Your grammar has a Mandarin accent

Three specific things, and they travel together.

The missing "is" and "are". You drop the verb "to be" before adjectives and before "-ing" forms. "She very tired." "I working now." What you almost never do is drop it before a noun, so "I am a student" comes out perfectly.

Narrow narrative tenses. Almost everything past comes out as past simple. The past continuous, the past perfect, "used to" and "would" are mostly missing, which flattens every story into a list of events.

Short, separate sentences. You produce a lot of short clauses placed next to each other, with the connection between them left for the listener to work out.

Why all three happen, and why they are one problem, not three: Mandarin does not use a copula before adjectives. "I very happy" is correct Mandarin, and adding "is" there would be wrong. But Mandarin does require one before a noun. That is exactly the split I heard in your English, which tells me this is one rule transferring across, not carelessness. Mandarin also marks aspect rather than tense, so English tense has to be learned rather than transferred. And Mandarin prefers placing clauses side by side and letting meaning connect them, where English signals the connection out loud.
What that means for you: your errors are systematic, which is far better news than random. A systematic error can be trained out with a rule and a drill. I can predict which sentences you will get wrong before you say them, and that is what makes them quick to fix.
The one idea to take away

Give, not get

If you remember nothing else from this document, remember these three words. They are the fix for the first problem above, and they quietly help the other two.

Right now, while you are speaking, part of your mind is asking a question: what will this get me? A better word. A higher band. A nod from the examiner. That question feels like effort, so it feels like it must be helping. It is not. It runs at the same time as the sentence you are building, using the same attention, and the sentence is what suffers.

Give is the opposite question: what am I giving this person? A picture. A story. A reason. Something they did not have thirty seconds ago.

It frees your attention

Hunting for an impressive word and building a clean sentence compete for the same limited pool. Stop hunting and the attention goes back into the sentence, which then comes out whole.

It lowers your nerves

Anxiety in a language test tracks how much attention you are aiming at yourself. Aim it at the listener instead and there is less of you to be nervous about.

It picks better words

When you are giving, you choose the word that carries your meaning most precisely, which is exactly what the top of the scoresheet asks for.

It makes your voice move

Nobody is monotone when they actually want someone to understand something. Caring about the message fixes some of the flatness for free.

Say this to yourself before you answer. Not "what is a good word for this", but "what do I want them to see". Then say the thing you want them to see, in whatever words arrive. We will train the words separately, at a time when you are not also trying to speak.
One honest caveat, because you deserve the real picture. Examiners genuinely do reward less common vocabulary. Your instinct to reach for it is not wrong, and I am not asking you to speak in small words. What is wrong is the timing. You are paying for the word in the middle of the sentence, at the most expensive possible moment. So we move that cost earlier: the phrases get learned and drilled until they are automatic, and then they arrive on their own while you are busy giving. Same ambition, different moment.
Ten minutes a day

Waking up your voice

Six drills. They feel ridiculous, they work fast, and you must do them where nobody can hear you or you will not commit properly and they will not work.

01

The siren

What to do: on an "ooo" or a hum, slide your voice slowly from the lowest note you can make to the highest, then back down. Like an ambulance in slow motion. Four or five times.

Why: almost every speaker uses a small fraction of the range they actually own. The siren is not training new notes, it is showing you the ones already there.

What changes: within a week your normal speech starts sitting in a wider band without you consciously doing anything, because your voice stops treating one note as the only safe place.

02

The children's story

What to do: read a children's picture book out loud, doing the voices. Properly. The wolf, the small frightened rabbit, the narrator. Two minutes.

Why: children's books are written to be performed, so they demand pitch movement you would never risk in a normal conversation. The permission to exaggerate is the point.

What changes: exaggeration in practice becomes ordinary variation in speech. You will feel like you are overdoing it by a mile. Recordings show you are not.

03

One line, five feelings

What to do: take any sentence. "I did not say she took the money." Say it five times: excited, bored, suspicious, delighted, exhausted. Same words every time.

Why: it proves that the words are not the message. Five identical sentences, five different meanings, and the only thing that changed was the melody.

What changes: you stop hearing intonation as decoration and start hearing it as meaning, which is the moment you can begin choosing it on purpose.

04

The cork

What to do: hold a wine cork, or the barrel of a pen, gently between your front teeth. Read a paragraph out loud for two minutes, pushing to stay clear. Take it out and read the same paragraph again.

Why: the obstruction forces your lips, tongue and jaw to work far harder than normal for the same clarity.

What changes: the second reading feels effortless, and your voice carries further with less push. Your sounds are already clear, so treat this one as a warm-up rather than a fix.

05

The deliberate pause

What to do: take a sentence and put one full second of silence immediately before its most important word. Feel how long a second is. It is longer than you think and shorter than you fear.

Why: silence is the only emphasis tool that costs no breath, no volume and no vocabulary. It is also the one learners avoid most, because silence feels like failure when it is actually control.

What changes: you get emphasis without shouting, and you buy real thinking time that sounds deliberate instead of stuck. For you specifically, this converts your hesitation from a weakness into a feature.

06

The playback

What to do: record sixty seconds of yourself speaking. Do not listen today. Listen tomorrow.

Why: you cannot hear yourself accurately while you speak, because your own skull conducts the sound to your ears differently from how it reaches everyone else. Waiting a day removes the memory of what you meant to sound like.

What changes: the gap between how you think you sound and how you do. Closing that gap is the single fastest source of improvement available to you, and it costs nothing.

How to fit this in. All six take about ten minutes together. Do them in the car, in the shower, or in a room with the door closed. There is a block for exactly this in the planner below, and if you tick only one block on the whole page, tick that one.

The part that decides everything

Finding the time you already have

Most students tell me they have no time. Almost none of them are right. What they have is no free time, which is a different problem with a different solution.

You do not need an empty hour. You need the moments that are already happening: the drive, the queue, the dishes, the ten minutes before you fall asleep. Those moments are not empty, but most of them leave one part of you free. That free part is where English goes.

Your voice is free

Driving alone, cooking, showering, walking. Your mouth is doing nothing. For you this is the most valuable kind by a distance, because your voice drills need it and so does the three-times drill.

Your ears are free

The gym, the commute, chores. Your eyes and hands are busy but your ears are not. Listening time, and for you, listening for where other people's voices move.

Your eyes are free

Queues, waiting rooms, the bus, before bed. Short and unpredictable, which makes it perfect for phrases in small doses.

Everything is free

A lunch break alone, a quiet weekend hour. Rare, so do not waste it on something the other slots could have covered. This is writing time.

    Why the plan looks like this

    The evidence behind all of it

    These are not my opinions, and none of these findings are about you personally. They are about thousands of learners, many of them Mandarin speakers with exactly your profile.

    Why your words cost you fluency

    Attention is a fixed pool, and vocabulary hunting spends it

    When researchers measured learners doing the same speaking task repeatedly, those reaching for elaborate vocabulary produced measurably more filled pauses, and those using more complex sentence structures made significantly more errors. Complexity, accuracy and fluency compete. You cannot raise one without the others falling, until the material becomes familiar enough to stop costing anything.

    So your plan: phrases get drilled to automatic before you need them, so speaking them costs nothing.
    Why we repeat things

    By the third attempt, the trade-off disappeared entirely

    In the same study, all the negative relationships between complexity, accuracy and fluency vanished by the third repetition of the task. Familiar material draws on information that is already primed, which takes less effort to reach, which hands your attention back.

    So your plan: you will say the same answers more than once, on purpose. This is not laziness in the lesson design. It is the mechanism.
    The strongest result in the field

    Saying the same thing three times, faster each time

    Learners deliver the same short talk three times, with four minutes, then three, then two. In a controlled study the group doing this moved from 7.83 to 11.00 on a twelve point fluency scale, while the control group moved from 8.00 to 8.25. The effect size was 2.65, which is very large for anything in language teaching.

    So your plan: the three-times drill, from Week 3, while you cook.
    Why your voice stays flat

    The pitch skill is already there. It has not crossed over yet.

    Native English speakers use a significantly wider pitch range to mark the important word in a sentence. In one study, Mandarin-speaking learners of English did not do this at all, even when the target word was shown to them in bold, and instead used loudness, which native speakers do not use for that purpose. The same speakers do use pitch this way in Mandarin.

    So your plan: the drills do not teach you a new ability. They connect one you already own to a language it has not been used in.
    Why the flatness costs marks

    The pronunciation criterion is built on the word "range"

    Band 6 is described as using a range of pronunciation features with mixed control. Band 8 is a wide range of pronunciation features. Intonation is one of those features. Clear consonants alone cannot carry you up that ladder if your pitch never moves.

    So your plan: ten minutes of voice work a day, from day one, because it is cheap and it moves a criterion you are currently capped on.
    Why you drop "is" and "are"

    One Mandarin rule, transferring exactly where you would predict

    Mandarin does not use a copula before adjectives. "I very happy" is correct Mandarin, and inserting the equivalent of "is" would be an error. Before a noun, Mandarin does require one. That predicts precisely the pattern in your English: the verb goes missing before adjectives and "-ing" forms, and stays put before nouns.

    So your plan: one rule, one drill, one measured count in week 2 and again in week 4.
    Why your sentences stay short

    Two languages, two ways of joining ideas

    Mandarin commonly places clauses side by side and leaves the reader to infer the relationship. English states the relationship out loud with a connector or a subordinate clause. Research tracking Chinese university students found that growth in written complexity moves in stages, from joined clauses to subordination to denser noun phrases, which means this is a developmental route with known steps rather than a fixed ceiling.

    So your plan: week 3 works on saying the connection aloud, starting with narrative, because stories make the joins obvious.
    Why chunks, not words

    Fixed phrases are processed faster, for non-native speakers too

    Learners read set phrases measurably faster than equivalent phrases assembled word by word, and non-native speakers get the same advantage native speakers do. Separately, comparing Taiwanese learners with native speakers, one study found learners produced 639 different collocation types against 1,628, yet used collocations at a higher rate per thousand words. The issue is not quantity. It is range.

    So your plan: everything I send you is phrases. A word you know must be assembled. A phrase you know arrives whole.
    Why give, not get

    Anxiety and achievement are consistently, moderately negatively related

    Across 97 studies and nearly 20,000 learners in 23 countries, higher language anxiety went with lower achievement, at a correlation of -0.36. That is not a small effect, and it means your nerves are part of your score whether or not anyone teaches them.

    So your plan: we move your attention off yourself and onto the person listening, and we rehearse under conditions that feel like the real room.
    Why out loud

    Reading aloud improves memory for the material, but not understanding of it

    Across four experiments, reading passages aloud beat reading silently on memory questions every time, and made no difference at all on comprehension questions. Both halves of that matter.

    So your plan: say your phrases out loud, because that is what makes them stick. But do not mistake reading aloud for studying.
    How I teach

    My approach, in six lines

    If you only remember six things about how these lessons work, remember these.

    Fluency is not new language. It is faster access to the language you already have.

    This is your situation exactly. You are not short of English. You are paying too much to reach it, at the worst possible moment. That is a practice problem, not a knowledge problem, which is why the plan repeats things rather than adding things.

    Meaning rides on content words. Grammar scaffolds the rest.

    Nouns, verbs and adjectives carry your message. Grammar holds them in place. When time is short, protect the words that carry meaning first, then tidy the scaffolding.

    Phrases, never single words.

    A word learned alone must be assembled under pressure. A phrase learned whole arrives ready. Everything I send you is built this way.

    Errors that repeat get fixed. Errors that happen once get ignored.

    Examiners notice patterns, not slips. So I listen for the mistake you make three times, and I only ever hand you three things at once. You have three. That is the whole list.

    You are scored on the English, not the idea.

    There is no mark for being interesting, being right, or being truthful. Once you accept that, you stop hunting for the perfect answer and start choosing the answer you can say best.

    Speak like you speak to your best friend.

    Trying to sound academic is what put you in this position. Natural, complete, accurate sentences score higher than stiff, impressive ones, and they are far easier to produce when you are nervous.
