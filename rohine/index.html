What is costing you marks

Every item below has a cause, and in almost every case the cause is that Mandarin does something sensible that English does not allow. That matters, because a random error is a problem and a systematic one is a schedule.

1

The same safe words on rotationMost of my students

Lexical resource · A small set, used often and correctly
Examples from my students:

"The food is very good." "It is very convenient." "It is a very important thing." "There are a lot of people." Four answers, four topics, one vocabulary. Nothing there is wrong, which is the problem: the range never gets a chance to show.

Why it happens

This is a rational strategy under pressure: you use what you are certain about. Corpus work finds learners actually produce more word pairs per thousand words than native speakers do, from roughly a third as many different types. The instinct to bind words together is strong. The pool is narrow.

What it costs

Lexical resource is a quarter of the marking and it is measured on range and precision together. Accurate and narrow sits in the middle of that quarter.

What to do about it
  1. Ban your three most used adjectives for one week. The replacements arrive quickly.
  2. Keep a list of words you understood while reading but would not have produced. That list is your real range.
  3. Once per answer, replace a general word with the specific noun underneath it.
2

Memorised answersMany of my students

Lexical resource · Prepared scripts that stop working
Examples from my students:

An answer arrives fully formed: "There are numerous reasons why I hold this view, and I would like to elaborate on two of them in particular." The follow up question then gets "Yes. I think so. Maybe." The distance between those two answers is the thing an examiner notices, and it is the second one they believe.

Why it happens

Memorising is a rational response to fear, and to an education that rewarded reproducing correct text. The problem is that it is audible: rhythm, vocabulary and fluency all change at the seam, and the drop afterwards is more visible than the polish before it.

What it costs

Examiners are trained to hear this and the answer is discounted. It also removes the chance to score on the one thing memorising cannot fake, which is responding.

What to do about it
  1. Memorise ideas and examples, never sentences.
  2. Prepare five personal stories that can be bent to fit fifty questions.
  3. Practise answering questions you have not seen, badly, on purpose.
3

Speaking too fastMany of my students

Fluency and coherence · Speed used to cover uncertainty
Examples from my students:

On a familiar topic: steady, clear, accurate. On "How has education changed in the last fifty years?" the pace doubles and the same speaker produces "the education is very different because the technology make people can study everything." Speed went up, accuracy went down, in the same breath.

Why it happens

Speed feels like fluency and is not. Rushing removes the planning time your grammar needs, so the harder the question the more the speed costs.

What it costs

It damages fluency and grammar together, which is the worst trade available.

What to do about it
  1. One breath, one sentence.
  2. Deliberate two second pauses at commas.
  3. Slow to eighty percent on Part 3 and watch accuracy rise.
4

Relative clausesMany of my students

Grammatical range and accuracy · Which, who and that doing no work
Examples from my students:

"I have a friend. He lives in Kaohsiung. He is a teacher." Three correct sentences doing the work of one: "I have a friend who teaches in Kaohsiung."

Why it happens

Mandarin puts the describing part before the noun and marks it with 的 de, so "the friend who lives in Kaohsiung" is built in the opposite order and needs no relative pronoun at all. English reverses the order and adds a word that has no equivalent in your first language. Producing one means running the sentence backwards while you speak.

What it costs

This is one of the specific structures examiners listen for when marking range. Its absence caps that half of the quarter even when accuracy is perfect.

What to do about it
  1. Join two of your own sentences with who or which, once per answer.
  2. Drill the pattern out loud: a place where, a time when, the person who, the thing that.
  3. Describe one photo a day in single sentences containing a relative clause.
5

V and WSome of my students

Pronunciation · Village and window swapping
Examples from my students:

"I live in a small willage." "Please close the vindow." "Wery good." "Wisit my friend." The two swap places in both directions.

Why it happens

In Mandarin the v sound exists only as a variant of w. Swapping them never changes a meaning, so they were never stored as two different things. English treats them as separate words and expects you to keep them apart.

What it costs

Occasionally blocks a word, which makes it worth a few minutes even though the number of affected words is small.

What to do about it
  1. V touches the teeth to the lip. W does not touch at all. Feel the difference rather than listening for it.
  2. Ten pairs a day: vine wine, vest west, veil wail.
6

Never taught out loudMost of my students

Pronunciation · English learned as a written subject
Examples from my students:

A student writes a clean conditional sentence with a relative clause inside it, and then says "I very like it" out loud thirty seconds later. The knowledge is all in there. Most of it has simply never been out of the mouth before.

Why it happens

This is a syllabus outcome, not an ability one. Exam focused instruction leaves learners largely confined to English on paper, with little systematic pronunciation teaching, and studies of learners from this background name that directly as the reason speaking lags. If you have never been asked to say a word out loud, the fact that you cannot say it smoothly tells you nothing about your ability. It tells you about the lessons.

What it costs

It makes every other item on this list look worse than it is, because it puts your visible performance below your actual knowledge.

What to do about it
  1. Read aloud daily. Anything. This is the single highest return habit for someone in your position.
  2. Say new words out loud the first time you meet them, before you write them down.
  3. Move material you already know from the page into your mouth. That is a much shorter job than learning it.

The play

Tool 9. Homework for Life

Adapted from Matthew Dicks·Storyworthy·60-time Moth StorySLAM champion

Exercise 04, page 8 of the workbookMoves: Fluency and coherence, and lexical resource
Why this one, for you

Rohine, I took this off finding 2 above, memorised answers, because the polished answers are not the problem. The empty shelf behind them is. This builds the shelf, one line a night, so that the next time a cue card lands you are choosing between real memories instead of reaching for a script.

What the play is

A memorised answer is not a memory problem. It is a supply problem. You reached for a script because when the question arrived, the shelf behind you was empty, and an empty shelf under sixty seconds of pressure will make anyone reach for something borrowed.

Dicks fills the shelf with one line a night. Not a diary, not a paragraph. One sentence naming the most story-like thing that happened that day, written before sleep. Two things happen over a few weeks. You build a searchable bank of real moments, and, more importantly, you retrain your attention: once you know you owe the notebook a line tonight, you start noticing candidates during the day. Consciously processed experience is far more retrievable later, which is exactly what a one minute preparation window needs.

What it does to the score is direct. A borrowed story is stored as a summary, and under adrenaline the summary is all you have, so it runs dry around forty seconds and the examiner hears the seam. A real memory is stored with its detail attached, so the description arrives without effort and drags precise vocabulary in with it. That is fluency and lexical resource from one habit, and it removes the single thing examiners are trained to detect and cap you for.

Try tonight, before anything else

Write one line naming the most story-like five seconds of today. Format it as a moment, not a topic: not "I went to work" but "the moment the woman in front of me paid for the stranger behind her". Then say it out loud once. That is the whole first rep, and it takes under a minute.

That is one worked example, and it is yours to keep whatever you decide next.

Waking up your voice

Five minutes, every morning, before any other English. These are not warm ups for the test. They change the range your voice has available, and a wider range is scored in a quarter of the marking that costs no vocabulary and no grammar.

1The countdown

Answer the same question three times, in ninety seconds, then sixty, then forty five. Same content, less time.

Why it works. Fixing the content forces all the improvement into delivery. Speed rises and pausing falls without you thinking about either.

2The deliberate pause

Read a paragraph and stop completely for two full seconds at every comma. Do not fill the gap with any sound.

Why it works. Silence feels much longer to the speaker than to the listener. This trains pauses that sound like thinking rather than like stalling, and it removes the pressure that produces filler noises.

3The one breath rule

Answer a question in one sentence per breath. When the breath ends, the sentence ends. Take a new breath and start a new one.

Why it works. It stops sentences running on until they collapse, which is the most common cause of grammar breaking down mid answer.

4The slow motion sentence

Say one sentence at half speed, deliberately hitting every ending sound, then say it at normal speed.

Why it works. Endings are where accuracy is won or lost. Slow motion makes them audible to you, and normal speed keeps some of that clarity.

There are more of these, and the order they are done in matters. That is a first lesson conversation.

Why this plan looks like this

Not opinion. These are the findings the plan is built on, and they are the reason it asks for twenty minutes daily rather than three hours on Sunday.

You already use more word pairs than native speakers do

A corpus comparison found second language learners producing 31.3 collocations per thousand words against 19.4 for native speakers. The learners used more, drawn from a much smaller set: 639 different types against 1628. So the issue is not that you avoid word partnerships. It is that you rotate a small number of safe ones. That is a range problem, and range builds faster than a habit.

Tsai, reported in Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019

Word partners do not fix themselves as your level rises

In a study drawing on ten thousand learner compositions from students with your language background, middle school writers produced 79 percent correct collocations and university writers 83 percent. The difference was not statistically significant. Accuracy with word partners does not improve just because everything else does, which is why it has to be worked on directly rather than waited out.

Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019, TECCL corpus

Fluent speakers reuse whole chunks

A large share of natural speech is built from ready made sequences rather than assembled word by word. Learners who store more of these sound faster and pause in more natural places, because they are retrieving instead of constructing.

Anxiety costs a real band, and it is trainable

Reviews across many studies find a consistent negative relationship between speaking anxiety and speaking performance. Anxiety occupies the working memory that grammar needs. Rehearsal under realistic conditions lowers it, because most of the fear is fear of the unfamiliar.

You were taught English as a written subject

Exam focused instruction leaves learners confined largely to English on paper, with little systematic pronunciation teaching. If your reading and grammar knowledge run well ahead of your speaking, that is the predictable outcome of the syllabus you sat, not a measure of your ability.

Wu, 2022

Attention is a fixed budget

Speaking research describes a trade off between three things you cannot all maximise at once: how complex your language is, how accurate it is, and how fluent it sounds. Push hard on one and the other two drop. This is not a personal weakness. It is how attention works under time pressure.

Range is measured in clauses, not in long words

Studies tracking learner development find growth shows up as more subordination and more varied clause types, not as longer vocabulary. Two ideas joined properly score above two ideas placed side by side.
