On wanting it to be right

You care about getting it correct, and that care is why your accuracy is where it is. It is also, in a specific way, costing you marks right now.

In speaking, the pursuit of a perfect sentence is expensive. Attention is a fixed budget shared between complexity, accuracy and fluency, and pushing hard on accuracy takes the resource the other two need. This is a documented trade off, not a failure of effort.

The most visible version of this is repair. A sentence starts, a small error appears, and the sentence restarts. The restart is scored as a break in fluency, and it usually costs more than the error it was fixing. One repair, then continue, is the rule.

The second version is silence. Waiting for the right word produces a gap, and a gap is scored as hesitation while a slightly imperfect word is not scored at all in most cases.

So part of the work is permission. Smaller sentences, finished. Ordinary words, used exactly. The ambition goes into the second sentence of an answer, never the first.

Your test is on 1 March 2027. From your trial lesson that is 25 weeks, or 181 days.

What is costing you marks

Every item below has a cause, and in almost every case the cause is that Mandarin does something sensible that English does not allow. That matters, because a random error is a problem and a systematic one is a schedule.

1

Concrete detail

Vocabulary · General words where specifics score
What I heard

Answers stay at the level of "it is very convenient" and "the environment is good" without the specific thing that makes it convenient or good.

Why it happens

General adjectives are safe and always available. Specific nouns are riskier and score higher, so the safe choice is the expensive one.

What it costs

Vocabulary is judged on precision. Three general adjectives score below one exact noun.

What to do about it
  1. Add one number, one name or one place to every answer.
  2. Replace "good" and "convenient" with the reason underneath them.
  3. Practise the follow up sentence: "For example, last week..."
2

Answers that stop too early

Fluency · Correct, complete, and too short
What I heard

You answer the question, accurately, and then stop, because the question has been answered.

Why it happens

This is a conversational norm, not a language gap. In Mandarin conversation, giving a short answer and letting the other person continue is polite and appropriate. The test inverts that rule: it wants the long turn, and silence is read as inability rather than as courtesy.

What it costs

Fluency and coherence is a quarter of the score and it is partly measured by sustained speech. A short answer gives the examiner nothing to score.

What to do about it
  1. Three part shape for every Part 3 answer: position, reason, example.
  2. Add "for example" out loud even when it feels unnecessary.
  3. Practise talking for forty five seconds on a boring topic on purpose.

Why this plan looks like this

Not opinion. These are the findings the plan is built on, and they are the reason it asks for twenty minutes daily rather than three hours on Sunday.

Answers need a shape

Research on learners from a Mandarin or Cantonese background finds a pattern of stating a position and then moving on, where higher scoring answers state, support, and illustrate. The knowledge is there. The habit of unpacking it is what is missing.

What the marking actually rewards

Speaking is scored on four equal parts: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. Each is a quarter of the score. A brilliant word list cannot lift a quarter it does not touch, and a broken quarter drags the whole result down.

Your first language is not the enemy

Every predictable error in this report has a cause in the structure of your first language. That is what makes it predictable, and predictable is exactly what can be trained. A random error is a problem. A systematic one is a schedule.

Spread it out, do not stack it

Reviews of hundreds of studies find the same thing: practice spread across days beats the same total time in one block, often by a wide margin. Twenty minutes on six days does more than two hours on Sunday.

Saying it out loud marks it

Material that is spoken aloud is remembered better than material read silently. The act of producing it makes it distinctive. Silent reading feels efficient and is not.
