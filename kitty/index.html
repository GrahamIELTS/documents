How I will work with you

You are steady about the test, which makes this straightforward. The plan can go directly at the things that are costing marks.

Three things shape how I teach. The first: every error in this report has a cause, usually in the structure of your first language, and I will always tell you the cause rather than just the correction. A rule you understand survives pressure. A rule you memorised does not.

The second: practice is spread out, not stacked. Twenty minutes on six days does more than two hours on one, and this is one of the most reliable findings in learning research.

The third: you hear yourself. Not because it is pleasant, but because you cannot hear your own speech while producing it, and change begins with noticing the gap.

What is costing you marks

Every item below has a cause, and in almost every case the cause is that Mandarin does something sensible that English does not allow. That matters, because a random error is a problem and a systematic one is a schedule.

1

The same safe words on rotation

Lexical resource · A small set, used often and correctly
What I heard

Good, important, convenient, a lot of. Nothing is wrong. The same twenty words carry every answer, so the range never shows.

Why it happens

This is a rational strategy under pressure: you use what you are certain about. Corpus work finds learners actually produce more word pairs per thousand words than native speakers do, from roughly a third as many different types. The instinct to bind words together is strong. The pool is narrow.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Lexical resource is a quarter of the marking and it is measured on range and precision together. Accurate and narrow sits in the middle of that quarter.

What to do about it
  1. Ban your three most used adjectives for one week. The replacements arrive quickly.
  2. Keep a list of words you understood while reading but would not have produced. That list is your real range.
  3. Once per answer, replace a general word with the specific noun underneath it.
2

Concrete detail

Lexical resource · General words where specifics score
What I heard

The specifics are there. What is missing is the sentence that says what they prove. See the finding on making the connection visible, which is the more accurate description of what I heard.

Why it happens

General adjectives are safe and always available. Specific nouns are riskier and score higher, so the safe choice is the expensive one.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Vocabulary is judged on precision. Three general adjectives score below one exact noun.

What to do about it
  1. Add one number, one name or one place to every answer.
  2. Replace "good" and "convenient" with the reason underneath them.
  3. Practise the follow up sentence: "For example, last week..."
3

Making the connection visible

Fluency and coherence · Good details, with the link left for the listener
What I heard

This one is worth reading carefully because it looks like a strength and is scored as a gap. Take an answer like: "Taipei is incredibly convenient. There are two convenience stores near my home. I can take a bus or the MRT almost anywhere." Every sentence is true, specific and well chosen. The claim is there, the evidence is there, and the sentence that ties them together is not. You are asking the listener to work out that two shops and two transport options are what convenient means to you. An examiner will make that connection, and will not give you credit for it, because you did not make it.

Why it happens

This is a rhetorical norm, not a thinking gap, and it is one of the best documented differences between how an argument is built in your first language and in English. In that tradition the listener is expected to do part of the work of reaching the meaning, and spelling something out too plainly can read as an insult, because it leaves the other person no room. English runs the opposite contract: the burden of clarity sits with the speaker. Studies of argumentative writing by learners with your background find exactly this shape, evidence stacked in a list without the bridging sentence, and find that adding more evidence does not raise the score. Adding the bridge does.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

It sits in fluency and coherence, which is a full quarter. Coherence is not about connectors like "moreover". It is about whether the listener can see why one sentence follows another. Research on this specific pattern found that once a speaker can supply evidence at all, further marks come from explaining it rather than from adding more, so this is the highest value single change available to you.

What to do about it
  1. Use the three step order: claim, then the standard you are judging by, then the evidence. Close with one sentence of the form "which is what I mean by convenient".
  2. Practise the closing sentence alone. Take five of your own good answers and add only the last line to each.
  3. The test phrase: could a listener disagree with my conclusion because I never said it. If yes, say it.
4

Speaking too fast

Fluency and coherence · Speed used to cover uncertainty
What I heard

The pace rises when the topic is difficult, and accuracy falls at exactly the same moment.

Why it happens

Speed feels like fluency and is not. Rushing removes the planning time your grammar needs, so the harder the question the more the speed costs.

What it costs

It damages fluency and grammar together, which is the worst trade available.

What to do about it
  1. One breath, one sentence.
  2. Deliberate two second pauses at commas.
  3. Slow to eighty percent on Part 3 and watch accuracy rise.
5

Participle clauses

Grammatical range and accuracy · Having finished, walking home, built in 1920
What I heard

Every clause is a full clause with its own subject and verb. The compressed forms that English uses to join ideas quickly do not appear.

Why it happens

Mandarin joins clauses by putting them next to each other and letting the relationship be understood, so there is no equivalent of a reduced clause carrying its own tense information. English compresses instead, and the compressed form has to be learned as a pattern rather than derived.

What it costs

This is a genuine band 7 and above marker. It is not required at 6, so treat it as a stretch item unless you are aiming higher.

What to do about it
  1. Learn two frames only: "Having lived there for ten years, I..." and "Built in 1920, the station..."
  2. Convert one of your own two clause sentences per day into a participle version, in writing first.
  3. Do not chase this under exam pressure. Build it in writing, and it will appear in speech later.
6

Narrative tenses

Grammatical range and accuracy · Everything told in one tense
What I heard

Past stories are told mostly in the present, or in the simple past only, with no shift between what was happening and what happened.

Why it happens

Mandarin does not mark time on the verb. Time is set once, with a word like yesterday or with a particle such as 了, and the verb never changes afterwards. English asks you to re mark time on every single verb. That is a genuinely heavier load, not a harder idea.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Grammatical range is measured on variety, not only on correctness. A whole story in one tense caps that half no matter how accurate it is.

What to do about it
  1. Learn three tenses as one set for storytelling: I was doing, I did, I had done.
  2. Tell one true story a day, out loud, sixty seconds, in the past.
  3. Mark the frame first: "Last year, while I was living in..." then continue.
7

Countable and uncountable

Grammatical range and accuracy · Many informations, an advice
What I heard

Nouns English treats as uncountable get a plural or an article: informations, advices, a furniture.

Why it happens

Mandarin counts everything with a measure word, so the noun itself carries no information about whether it can be counted. English hides that property inside the noun and expects you to know it word by word. There is no rule to derive. It is a list.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Larger than it looks. When I sorted my own teaching records across 261 student documents, this was the single most widespread grammar pattern I correct, ahead of subject verb agreement and the missing BE verb. It also rises as topics get more abstract, so it lands hardest in Part 3 and Task 2, which is exactly where you are trying to score.

What to do about it
  1. Learn the twelve that matter: information, advice, news, research, equipment, furniture, luggage, knowledge, progress, traffic, weather, work.
  2. Use a measure phrase: a piece of advice, an item of luggage.
8

Sentences that do not close

Grammatical range and accuracy · Commas doing the work of full stops
What I heard

In writing, independent clauses are joined with commas and the sentence runs on. In speech, the same thing happens with and, and the answer never lands.

Why it happens

A sentence boundary in your first language is looser and a comma legitimately joins what English would separate. You are punctuating by a rule that is correct in your first language.

What it costs

In Task 2 this is marked directly under grammatical accuracy. In speaking it costs coherence, because the listener cannot hear where one idea ends.

What to do about it
  1. One idea, one full stop. Read your writing aloud and stop where you breathe.
  2. In speech, the one breath rule does the same job.
  3. If a comma joins two things that could stand alone, it needs a full stop or a joining word.
9

Weak forms

Pronunciation · Small words given full value
What I heard

To, of, and, for, can, are all pronounced fully and clearly, every time. English reduces them to almost nothing.

Why it happens

Mandarin has no reduced vowels, so there is no equivalent of the schwa to transfer. Every syllable arrives at full quality. English hides most of its grammar in exactly these hollowed out words, which is also why fast English is hard to hear.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

It is the other half of rhythm, and it works in both directions: producing full forms sounds heavy, and expecting full forms makes native speech harder to follow.

What to do about it
  1. Learn five reduced forms and use them: to becomes ta, of becomes uh, and becomes n, for becomes fer, can becomes kn.
  2. Practise the contrast in one sentence: I can go, where can is almost silent, against I CAN go, where it is not.
10

Final L

Pronunciation · Pool and poor sounding the same
What I heard

The l at the end of a word turns into a vowel, so pool moves towards poor and feel towards fear.

Why it happens

In Mandarin the l sound only occurs at the beginning of a syllable, never at the end, so the mouth has no stored shape for a final one and substitutes the nearest comfortable vowel. English also uses a different, darker l in that position than the one at the start of a word, so even the sound you do have is not the right version.

What it costs

Moderate, and it interacts with plural and past endings after an l, so it pays twice.

What to do about it
  1. Hold the final l for a full second in practice, tongue tip firmly up, then shorten it.
  2. Cork drill on word lists ending in l: feel, small, hotel, careful.

What you have that helps

These are not encouragement. They are findings from the same research the rest of this document is built on, and they are the reason the list above is shorter than it looks.

You use more word partnerships than native speakers

A corpus study found learners producing more collocations per thousand words than native speakers did, drawn from a smaller pool. You are not avoiding natural word pairs. You are recycling a safe set of them. Widening a pool is a much smaller job than building one.

You already speak for a listener

The habit of leaving space for the other person to draw their own conclusion is a real skill, and in your first language it is the polite and sophisticated choice. English simply runs on the opposite agreement, where the speaker carries the work of being clear. You are not learning to think better. You are learning which agreement you are working under.

You are doing all of this in your second language

Whatever band you are at, you are being examined on your ability to argue, give examples and handle abstract topics in a language that is not your first. Most people cannot do that at all. The gap in front of you is between you and a target score, not between you and competence.

Waking up your voice

Five minutes, every morning, before any other English. These are not warm ups for the test. They change the range your voice has available, and a wider range is scored in a quarter of the marking that costs no vocabulary and no grammar.

1The deliberate pause

Read a paragraph and stop completely for two full seconds at every comma. Do not fill the gap with any sound.

Why it works. Silence feels much longer to the speaker than to the listener. This trains pauses that sound like thinking rather than like stalling, and it removes the pressure that produces filler noises.

2The one breath rule

Answer a question in one sentence per breath. When the breath ends, the sentence ends. Take a new breath and start a new one.

Why it works. It stops sentences running on until they collapse, which is the most common cause of grammar breaking down mid answer.

3The countdown

Answer the same question three times, in ninety seconds, then sixty, then forty five. Same content, less time.

Why it works. Fixing the content forces all the improvement into delivery. Speed rises and pausing falls without you thinking about either.

4The shadow

Play thirty seconds of a native speaker and talk along one beat behind, copying the melody and rhythm, not the meaning.

Why it works. It installs English rhythm as a whole pattern instead of as a list of rules about individual sounds.

There are more of these, and the order they are done in matters. That is a first lesson conversation.

Why this plan looks like this

Not opinion. These are the findings the plan is built on, and they are the reason it asks for twenty minutes daily rather than three hours on Sunday.

You already use more word pairs than native speakers do

A corpus comparison found second language learners producing 31.3 collocations per thousand words against 19.4 for native speakers. The learners used more, drawn from a much smaller set: 639 different types against 1628. So the issue is not that you avoid word partnerships. It is that you rotate a small number of safe ones. That is a range problem, and range builds faster than a habit.

Tsai, reported in Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019

Word partners do not fix themselves as your level rises

In a study drawing on ten thousand learner compositions from students with your language background, middle school writers produced 79 percent correct collocations and university writers 83 percent. The difference was not statistically significant. Accuracy with word partners does not improve just because everything else does, which is why it has to be worked on directly rather than waited out.

Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019, TECCL corpus

The gap between having evidence and using it

A study of 542 timed argumentative essays by undergraduates with your language background found the pattern changes with level. Weaker texts are assertion heavy and example light. Middle texts have plenty of evidence but stack it in a list without the bridging sentence that says why it counts. Only the strongest texts make that link explicit. Once you can supply evidence, further marks come from explaining it, not from adding more.

Yang, 2025

What the marking actually rewards

Speaking is scored on four equal parts: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. Each is a quarter. A brilliant word list cannot lift a quarter it does not touch, and a broken quarter drags the whole result down.

Who carries the work of being clear

Research on the rhetorical tradition behind your first language describes a norm where the reader is expected to work to reach the meaning, and where stating something too explicitly can read as an insult, because it leaves the reader no room to think. English academic and test discourse runs on the opposite contract: the burden of clarity sits with the speaker. Neither is better. They are different agreements about who does the work, and the test is marked under the English one.

Chien, on Kaplan and Connor

Claim, frame, evidence

The same study proposes a three step order that fixes this directly. State the claim. Set the frame, meaning the standard by which it should be judged. Then give the evidence, and close with one sentence saying why that evidence supports that claim. The closing sentence is the one that is usually missing.

Yang, 2025

The shape of a good argument is not universal

The traditional pattern behind your first language builds from evidence towards a point, circling the topic and arriving at the thesis late, or leaving it to be inferred. That is a rhetorical tradition with five centuries of examination history behind it, not a mistake. English expects the point first and the evidence after. Learning the English shape does not mean the other one was wrong.

Chien, on Kaplan 1966 and Connor 1996
