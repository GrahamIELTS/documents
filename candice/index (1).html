On feeling nervous about this

You told me you feel nervous about the test. That is worth answering properly rather than brushing past, because nervousness is not a personality problem here. It is a measurable cost, and it is trainable.

Research across a large number of studies finds a consistent negative relationship between speaking anxiety and speaking performance. The mechanism is simple: anxiety occupies working memory, and grammar needs the same working memory. You are not less capable when you are nervous. You have less capacity free.

It is also documented rather than imagined. A study of learners with your language background found five themes running through their accounts of learning English, and two of them were emotional: anxiety and self doubt appeared as persistent obstacles alongside the language itself. There is a long standing observation in this field that learners from this background are often described as quiet and afraid of mistakes, of laughter, of being wrong in front of people, and that this counts against them in speaking assessments specifically. That is a description of how speaking was taught and marked. It is not a description of your English.

Four principles guide how I will approach speaking with you.

The first: speaking is not performing. It is paying attention to the person in front of you. Nervousness comes almost entirely from watching yourself. Move the attention onto the examiner, onto the actual question, onto what you are describing, and there is less of you left over to be nervous with.

The second: you do not need to win the conversation. You need to keep it alive. Every answer is a pass, not a shot. This removes the idea that any single sentence can ruin the test, which is the belief doing most of the damage.

The third: say the small true thing rather than the big impressive thing. The small true thing is always available, always accurate, and scores higher than the impressive sentence that collapses halfway.

The fourth: do not apologise before you begin. No "my English is not good", no nervous laugh before the answer. It costs you nothing to remove and it changes how the whole conversation is heard.

Practically, most of the fear is fear of the unfamiliar, and the unfamiliar is the only part I can remove directly. By the time you sit the test you will have done the format enough times that it is boring. Boring is the goal.

Your test is on 6 February 2027. From your trial lesson that is 22 weeks, or 158 days.

What is already working

Strong ideas

Specific, concrete detail

You reach for real examples rather than general adjectives. Numbers, places, things that actually happened. That is the raw material examiners are looking for, and most candidates do not bring it.

Natural conversational tone

Comfortable pace

Your speed is easy to follow, neither rushed nor slow. This is worth protecting when the other work starts.

What is costing you marks

Every item below has a cause, and in almost every case the cause is that Mandarin does something sensible that English does not allow. That matters, because a random error is a problem and a systematic one is a schedule.

1

Reaching for advanced vocabulary

Lexical resource · Big words slowing everything else down
What I heard

You stop mid sentence to search for an impressive word. The search is visible, the sentence loses its shape, and the word that arrives is often not the one that fits.

Why it happens

This is a taught habit rather than a language one. Study materials and word lists reward rarity, so effort goes there. The cost stays invisible until you are timed.

What it costs

It damages three of the four quarters at once. Fluency drops because of the pause, grammar drops because the sentence was abandoned, and vocabulary itself drops, because the marking rewards words used naturally and accurately rather than words that are rare. A rare word used slightly wrongly scores below a common word used exactly.

What to do about it
  1. Answer with the first word that is true. Precision beats rarity in the marking.
  2. Build partners, not words. Learn "make a decision", not "decision".
  3. Reserve ambition for the second sentence of an answer, never the first.
2

The same safe words on rotation

Lexical resource · A small set, used often and correctly
What I heard

Good, important, convenient, a lot of. Nothing is wrong. The same twenty words carry every answer, so the range never shows.

Why it happens

This is a rational strategy under pressure: you use what you are certain about. Corpus work finds learners actually produce more word pairs per thousand words than native speakers do, from roughly a third as many different types. The instinct to bind words together is strong. The pool is narrow.

What it costs

Lexical resource is a quarter of the marking and it is measured on range and precision together. Accurate and narrow sits in the middle of that quarter.

What to do about it
  1. Ban your three most used adjectives for one week. The replacements arrive quickly.
  2. Keep a list of words you understood while reading but would not have produced. That list is your real range.
  3. Once per answer, replace a general word with the specific noun underneath it.
3

Memorised answers

Lexical resource · Prepared scripts that stop working
What I heard

Some answers arrive complete and polished, then the next question breaks the script and the level drops sharply.

Why it happens

Memorising is a rational response to fear, and to an education that rewarded reproducing correct text. The problem is that it is audible: rhythm, vocabulary and fluency all change at the seam, and the drop afterwards is more visible than the polish before it.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

Examiners are trained to hear this and the answer is discounted. It also removes the chance to score on the one thing memorising cannot fake, which is responding.

What to do about it
  1. Memorise ideas and examples, never sentences.
  2. Prepare five personal stories that can be bent to fit fifty questions.
  3. Practise answering questions you have not seen, badly, on purpose.
4

Concrete detail

Lexical resource · General words where specifics score
What I heard

Answers stay at the level of "it is very convenient" and "the environment is good" without the specific thing that makes it convenient or good.

Why it happens

General adjectives are safe and always available. Specific nouns are riskier and score higher, so the safe choice is the expensive one.

What it costs

Vocabulary is judged on precision. Three general adjectives score below one exact noun.

What to do about it
  1. Add one number, one name or one place to every answer.
  2. Replace "good" and "convenient" with the reason underneath them.
  3. Practise the follow up sentence: "For example, last week..."
5

Making the connection visible

Fluency and coherence · Good details, with the link left for the listener
What I heard

This one is worth reading carefully because it looks like a strength and is scored as a gap. Take an answer like: "Taipei is incredibly convenient. There are two convenience stores near my home. I can take a bus or the MRT almost anywhere." Every sentence is true, specific and well chosen. The claim is there, the evidence is there, and the sentence that ties them together is not. You are asking the listener to work out that two shops and two transport options are what convenient means to you. An examiner will make that connection, and will not give you credit for it, because you did not make it.

Why it happens

This is a rhetorical norm, not a thinking gap, and it is one of the best documented differences between how an argument is built in your first language and in English. In that tradition the listener is expected to do part of the work of reaching the meaning, and spelling something out too plainly can read as an insult, because it leaves the other person no room. English runs the opposite contract: the burden of clarity sits with the speaker. Studies of argumentative writing by learners with your background find exactly this shape, evidence stacked in a list without the bridging sentence, and find that adding more evidence does not raise the score. Adding the bridge does.

Worth saying plainly: this is one of the most common things I hear from students who speak your first language. You are in the majority here, not the exception, and the majority is the group I have the most practice moving.

What it costs

It sits in fluency and coherence, which is a full quarter. Coherence is not about connectors like "moreover". It is about whether the listener can see why one sentence follows another. Research on this specific pattern found that once a speaker can supply evidence at all, further marks come from explaining it rather than from adding more, so this is the highest value single change available to you.

What to do about it
  1. Use the three step order: claim, then the standard you are judging by, then the evidence. Close with one sentence of the form "which is what I mean by convenient".
  2. Practise the closing sentence alone. Take five of your own good answers and add only the last line to each.
  3. The test phrase: could a listener disagree with my conclusion because I never said it. If yes, say it.
6

Over correcting

Fluency and coherence · Restarting sentences mid way
What I heard

A sentence begins, an error is noticed, and the sentence restarts. Sometimes twice.

Why it happens

The awareness is good and the timing is expensive. Every restart is scored as a break in fluency, and the original error was usually smaller than the repair. Learners who were taught in a system where a written mistake was the thing that cost marks tend to carry that reflex into speech, where the economics are reversed.

What it costs

A repaired sentence costs more than an uncorrected small error. Two repairs cost more than the error was ever worth.

What to do about it
  1. One repair rule: fix it once, then continue whatever happens.
  2. Finish the sentence you started, even badly.
  3. Save the correction for the recording, afterwards.
7

Relative clauses

Grammatical range and accuracy · Which, who and that doing no work
What I heard

Descriptions are delivered as separate sentences. "I have a friend. He lives in Kaohsiung. He is a teacher." Three correct sentences where English would use one.

Why it happens

Mandarin puts the describing part before the noun and marks it with 的 de, so "the friend who lives in Kaohsiung" is built in the opposite order and needs no relative pronoun at all. English reverses the order and adds a word that has no equivalent in your first language. Producing one means running the sentence backwards while you speak.

What it costs

This is one of the specific structures examiners listen for when marking range. Its absence caps that half of the quarter even when accuracy is perfect.

What to do about it
  1. Join two of your own sentences with who or which, once per answer.
  2. Drill the pattern out loud: a place where, a time when, the person who, the thing that.
  3. Describe one photo a day in single sentences containing a relative clause.
8

Conditionals

Grammatical range and accuracy · If I have money, I will buy
What I heard

The real conditional works. The unreal ones flatten into it, so hypothetical Part 3 questions get answered as though they were about the actual future.

Why it happens

Mandarin marks hypothetical meaning with words like 如果 and with context, not by shifting the tense backwards. English signals unreality by moving the verb into the past, which is counter intuitive if your first language never does it.

What it costs

Part 3 is full of hypothetical questions. Answering them in the wrong conditional both loses a range mark and slightly changes what you appear to be claiming.

What to do about it
  1. Drill the second conditional as a fixed frame: if I had, I would.
  2. Answer one "what would happen if" question a day out loud.
  3. Notice the trigger words: would, imagine, suppose. They all take the shifted tense.
9

Prepositions

Grammatical range and accuracy · In, on, at and the rest
What I heard

Prepositions are chosen by meaning rather than by the fixed pattern English uses.

Why it happens

Mandarin maps space and time onto a different set of words, and no rule transfers. In learner corpora prepositions account for around a tenth of collocation errors, which is real but small next to verb choice.

What it costs

Low individually and noticeable in bulk. Worth doing in the background, never as the main project.

What to do about it
  1. Learn them attached to the noun: on Monday, in July, at six.
  2. Fix the ten that appear most in your own speech and ignore the rest for now.
10

Sentences that do not close

Grammatical range and accuracy · Commas doing the work of full stops
What I heard

In writing, independent clauses are joined with commas and the sentence runs on. In speech, the same thing happens with and, and the answer never lands.

Why it happens

A sentence boundary in your first language is looser and a comma legitimately joins what English would separate. You are punctuating by a rule that is correct in your first language.

What it costs

In Task 2 this is marked directly under grammatical accuracy. In speaking it costs coherence, because the listener cannot hear where one idea ends.

What to do about it
  1. One idea, one full stop. Read your writing aloud and stop where you breathe.
  2. In speech, the one breath rule does the same job.
  3. If a comma joins two things that could stand alone, it needs a full stop or a joining word.
11

Connected speech

Pronunciation · Clean gaps where English runs words together
What I heard

Every word is produced separately and correctly, with an audible boundary between each one. Nothing is wrong and nothing joins.

Why it happens

The clearest description of this compares the two languages to music: Mandarin is staccato, where the boundary between syllables is meant to be heard, and English is legato, where the flow is continuous and the boundaries are deliberately hard to find. Another version of the same image is that a sentence in your first language is beads strung on a thread and an English sentence is water. You are producing beads, accurately, in a language that wants water.

What it costs

It reads as effortful rather than as incorrect, which caps the pronunciation quarter at the middle. It also hurts your listening, because native speakers are linking and you are listening for separate words.

What to do about it
  1. Learn three linking patterns: consonant into vowel (pick it up), vowel into vowel (go on), and the disappearing t (wan(t) to).
  2. Shadow audio and copy the joins rather than the words.
  3. Read one paragraph aloud daily with no gaps at all, deliberately overdone.
12

Weak forms

Pronunciation · Small words given full value
What I heard

To, of, and, for, can, are all pronounced fully and clearly, every time. English reduces them to almost nothing.

Why it happens

Mandarin has no reduced vowels, so there is no equivalent of the schwa to transfer. Every syllable arrives at full quality. English hides most of its grammar in exactly these hollowed out words, which is also why fast English is hard to hear.

What it costs

It is the other half of rhythm, and it works in both directions: producing full forms sounds heavy, and expecting full forms makes native speech harder to follow.

What to do about it
  1. Learn five reduced forms and use them: to becomes ta, of becomes uh, and becomes n, for becomes fer, can becomes kn.
  2. Practise the contrast in one sentence: I can go, where can is almost silent, against I CAN go, where it is not.
13

Voiced and voiceless endings

Pronunciation · Bag and back, seed and seat
What I heard

The final consonant in bag sounds like back, and seed like seat. The vowel before it is also shorter than English expects.

Why it happens

This is a whole feature your ear has never needed. English separates eight consonant pairs by voicing, whether the vocal cords are running: p and b, t and d, k and g, s and z, f and v, and so on. Mandarin separates its pairs by aspiration instead, by how much breath follows, and voicing never changes a meaning. So English is asking you to hear and produce a contrast that has been irrelevant your whole life. Native English speakers have the mirror version of this problem and cannot hear aspiration.

What it costs

Moderate on its own, and it compounds with the plural and past endings, so it pays twice.

What to do about it
  1. Feel it rather than hear it. Hand on the throat: buzzing for b, d, g, z, and nothing for p, t, k, s.
  2. Lengthen the vowel before a voiced ending. English marks bag mostly by making the a longer, not by the g.
  3. Ten pairs a day.
14

Final L

Pronunciation · Pool and poor sounding the same
What I heard

The l at the end of a word turns into a vowel, so pool moves towards poor and feel towards fear.

Why it happens

In Mandarin the l sound only occurs at the beginning of a syllable, never at the end, so the mouth has no stored shape for a final one and substitutes the nearest comfortable vowel. English also uses a different, darker l in that position than the one at the start of a word, so even the sound you do have is not the right version.

What it costs

Moderate, and it interacts with plural and past endings after an l, so it pays twice.

What to do about it
  1. Hold the final l for a full second in practice, tongue tip firmly up, then shorten it.
  2. Cork drill on word lists ending in l: feel, small, hotel, careful.

What you have that helps

These are not encouragement. They are findings from the same research the rest of this document is built on, and they are the reason the list above is shorter than it looks.

You use more word partnerships than native speakers

A corpus study found learners producing more collocations per thousand words than native speakers did, drawn from a smaller pool. You are not avoiding natural word pairs. You are recycling a safe set of them. Widening a pool is a much smaller job than building one.

The knowledge is already in you

You studied English for years as a written subject, with little systematic speaking or pronunciation teaching. That means what looks like a gap in ability is usually a gap in access: the grammar and vocabulary are already stored, they have just never been asked to come out at speed. Getting at what you already own is a far shorter job than acquiring it.

You already speak for a listener

The habit of leaving space for the other person to draw their own conclusion is a real skill, and in your first language it is the polite and sophisticated choice. English simply runs on the opposite agreement, where the speaker carries the work of being clear. You are not learning to think better. You are learning which agreement you are working under.

You are doing all of this in your second language

Whatever band you are at, you are being examined on your ability to argue, give examples and handle abstract topics in a language that is not your first. Most people cannot do that at all. The gap in front of you is between you and a target score, not between you and competence.

Waking up your voice

Five minutes, every morning, before any other English. These are not warm ups for the test. They change the range your voice has available, and a wider range is scored in a quarter of the marking that costs no vocabulary and no grammar.

1The countdown

Answer the same question three times, in ninety seconds, then sixty, then forty five. Same content, less time.

Why it works. Fixing the content forces all the improvement into delivery. Speed rises and pausing falls without you thinking about either.

2The one breath rule

Answer a question in one sentence per breath. When the breath ends, the sentence ends. Take a new breath and start a new one.

Why it works. It stops sentences running on until they collapse, which is the most common cause of grammar breaking down mid answer.

3The shadow

Play thirty seconds of a native speaker and talk along one beat behind, copying the melody and rhythm, not the meaning.

Why it works. It installs English rhythm as a whole pattern instead of as a list of rules about individual sounds.

4The story voice

Read one paragraph of a children’s book out loud, to an imaginary six year old who is losing interest. Exaggerate everything.

Why it works. It removes the fear of sounding excessive. Played back, the exaggerated version almost always sounds normal, which tells you how much room you actually have.

There are more of these, and the order they are done in matters. That is a first lesson conversation.

Why this plan looks like this

Not opinion. These are the findings the plan is built on, and they are the reason it asks for twenty minutes daily rather than three hours on Sunday.

Attention is a fixed budget

Speaking research describes a trade off between three things you cannot all maximise at once: how complex your language is, how accurate it is, and how fluent it sounds. Push hard on one and the other two drop. This is not a personal weakness. It is how attention works under time pressure.

What the marking actually rewards

Speaking is scored on four equal parts: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. Each is a quarter. A brilliant word list cannot lift a quarter it does not touch, and a broken quarter drags the whole result down.

You already use more word pairs than native speakers do

A corpus comparison found second language learners producing 31.3 collocations per thousand words against 19.4 for native speakers. The learners used more, drawn from a much smaller set: 639 different types against 1628. So the issue is not that you avoid word partnerships. It is that you rotate a small number of safe ones. That is a range problem, and range builds faster than a habit.

Tsai, reported in Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019

Fluent speakers reuse whole chunks

A large share of natural speech is built from ready made sequences rather than assembled word by word. Learners who store more of these sound faster and pause in more natural places, because they are retrieving instead of constructing.

Word partners do not fix themselves as your level rises

In a study drawing on ten thousand learner compositions from students with your language background, middle school writers produced 79 percent correct collocations and university writers 83 percent. The difference was not statistically significant. Accuracy with word partners does not improve just because everything else does, which is why it has to be worked on directly rather than waited out.

Gao, Zhu and Luo, 2019, TECCL corpus

Anxiety costs a real band, and it is trainable

Reviews across many studies find a consistent negative relationship between speaking anxiety and speaking performance. Anxiety occupies the working memory that grammar needs. Rehearsal under realistic conditions lowers it, because most of the fear is fear of the unfamiliar.

You were taught English as a written subject

Exam focused instruction leaves learners confined largely to English on paper, with little systematic pronunciation teaching. If your reading and grammar knowledge run well ahead of your speaking, that is the predictable outcome of the syllabus you sat, not a measure of your ability.

Wu, 2022
